Mitra-v2: Scaling synthetic pretraining for tabular foundation models
Expanding the mix of synthetic data used to train a tabular foundation model enables it to adapt to new tables without new models and lead the single-model field on the TabArena benchmark.

Much of the data that businesses run on lives in tables: the rows might represent customers, transactions, or interactions, and their “attributes” are listed in the columns. Predicting missing attributes — such as likelihood of customer retention or how much demand to expect next week — is one of the most common machine learning tasks in industry.
The standard tools for this job, such as gradient-boosted decision trees, produce models tailored to specific tables. Every new table means choosing an algorithm, engineering features, tuning hyperparameters, and setting up a processing pipeline. Tabular foundation models promise to end that cycle: one pretrained model reads the labeled rows of a previously unseen table and predicts the rest, with no training run in-between.
Last year we introduced
Mitra
, a tabular foundation model pretrained entirely on synthetic tables. It worked well on small and mid-sized tables but ran out of room on large ones, wide ones, and problems with many classes.
Today we're introducing Mitra-v2, the second generation of the model, for both classification and regression. We describe it in a
technical report
, and we've released versions fine-tuned for both classification and regression, along with the inference and fine-tuning code, under the Apache-2.0 license on Hugging Face.
Amazon Connect Customer
, which provides agentic-AI systems for core business functions, led the effort. Amazon Connect Customer is adopting Mitra-v2 for prediction on structured business and customer-experience data, such as estimating how likely a business-relevant event is to occur given a customer's interaction history and operational context.
On
TabArena
, a public benchmark of 51 real-world datasets, Mitra-v2 leads the single-model field. It produces a better result than Mitra-v1 on 32 of the 33 datasets where both models run natively, and it completes all 51 datasets within the benchmark's one-hour budget, which Mitra-v1 failed to do on more than a third of them. On
ScoringBench
, a public benchmark for regression models that predict a range of likely outcomes rather than a single number, fine-tuned Mitra-v2 ranks first.
Synthetic pretraining
A large language model is pretrained on huge volumes of data, so when you give it a few examples in a prompt, it can generalize from them with no additional training. Tabular foundation models work the same way. We pretrain Mitra-v2 on millions of synthetic prediction problems, and at deployment time the labeled rows of a real table serve as the examples. The model reads them and predicts the unlabeled rows, a technique called in-context learning.
Every pretraining table is synthetic: we use no real data, and in particular, no customer data. The model learns general principles about how columns relate to one another rather than the contents of any real dataset. The lesson of the original Mitra was that the quality and diversity of those synthetic tables are central to how well the model generalizes.
Mitra-v2 takes that lesson further. The architecture is unchanged: a Transformer that
attends both across the rows of a table and across its columns
. What has changed is the size of the examples the model sees during pretraining. Mitra-v2 is pretrained on tables with 10 times as many rows and roughly three times as many columns as those used to train Mitra-v1, so it has seen tables the size of the ones businesses actually use.
We also added a new family of synthetic tables. To train the original Mitra, we generated tables from structural causal models (SCMs), which are graphs of cause-and-effect relationships between columns, and from tree-based models like the ones data scientists fit in practice. Mitra-v2's new hybrid SCM prior builds a single causal graph in which each relationship can be a small neural network, a decision tree, a smooth function drawn from a Gaussian process, or a time-dependent autoregressive process, so one synthetic table can carry the mix of relationships found in real tables. A new hybrid optimizer keeps training stable across this wider range.
Overview of Mitra-v2. Top: the mixture of synthetic pretraining priors, with the classical structural causal model (SCM), gradient boosting, and forest generators alongside the new hybrid SCM prior, which composes neural, tree, Gaussian-process, and autoregressive mechanisms inside a single causal graph. Bottom: the scaled-up pretraining envelope, the two-dimensional attention backbone carried over from Mitra-v1, and the hybrid optimizer.
At deployment time, two lightweight wrappers extend the model beyond its pretraining limits. For very wide tables, we use singular-value decomposition to produce a new table that fits in the allotted space, populated with synthetic values that capture most of the variance in the original data. Mitra-v2’s output layer has only 10 nodes, corresponding to 10 classes, so for tasks with more classes than that, we send each node’s output toa a hierarchical tree, which further refines the classification.
By default, Mitra-v2 is briefly, automatically fine-tuned on each new table it sees, producing eight copies whose predictions are averaged, a standard technique called bagging. So it isn't quite a zero-shot model. The adaptation adds some computational overhead, but in return, it delivers higher accuracy and gives customers additional options.
Results
We evaluated Mitra-v2 on TabArena, a living benchmark maintained by an independent academic team. TabArena ranks methods by Elo, the rating system used in chess, here computed from head-to-head wins across datasets under a fixed one-hour budget. Mitra-v2 leads the single-model field, as shown below, with large gains over Mitra-v1 on both classification and regression. It also ranks among the leading methods on the independent
TALENT
benchmark of roughly 300 datasets, where it's strongest on classification tasks with more than 10 classes. Full results, including per-dataset comparisons, are in the technical report.
Elo ratings of Mitra-v2 and the leading tabular foundation models on the TabArena boards (top) and the TALENT boards (bottom), September 2026.
For regression, Mitra-v2 predicts a full range of likely values for each row rather than a single number, so users get prediction intervals along with the point estimate. That’s the approach that ScoringBench evaluates, rewarding forecasts that are both accurate and well calibrated. On the ScoringBench leaderboard, fine-tuned Mitra-v2 again ranks first.
Mitra-v2 shows that a tabular foundation model can make substantial gains by widening the diversity and range of its synthetic pretraining, without a deeper network. We expect richer synthetic priors and adaptive mixtures to push this further. The fine-tuned classification and regression models and code are open on Hugging Face and ready to use, and we invite researchers and practitioners to try Mitra-v2 on their own tables.

About this role We're looking for a Data Scientist to turn data from our satellite fleet into engineering decisions. Every satellite in orbit sends back a steady stream of telemetry. You'll find out which in-orbit behaviors trace back to what we measured in the factory, which events repeat across the fleet, and which patterns point to weaknesses in the hardware design. Your findings will directly shape how we design, build, test, and operate the next generation of satellites. A typical problem starts small: a handful of satellites show a drifting temperature, an occasional link dropout, or a counter that creeps up faster than expected. You'll work out whether it's a real effect or noise, whether it's spreading, and what the affected units have in common. They might share a manufacturing lot, a configuration, an orbital environment, or a signature in their factory test history. From there you'll form a hypothesis about the physical cause and argue it in front of hardware, firmware, and reliability engineers who will push back hard. You'll also have to say plainly when the data rules your own idea out. Telemetry alone rarely settles the question, so your first stop is the ground test data we already have. Every unit in orbit has a detailed record from manufacturing and environmental testing, and the fleet as a whole is backed by an even larger population of units still on the ground. You'll dig through that history for the same behavior: did the affected units show early signs on the ground, did they sit near a test limit, does the effect appear across many units under certain conditions? Often the answer is already in data no one has looked at in this light. When it isn't, you'll design the ground experiments that can provide it. That means deciding which units to test and under what conditions, how many runs you need before the result means anything, and what outcome would confirm or refute the theory. You'll work with the engineers who run those tests, analyze the results, and close the question with a clear answer and a recommended action. Beyond individual investigations, you'll build the fleet-level view: baselines for healthy behavior, early warning indicators that catch emerging issues before they cause outages, and reliability estimates that tell us how the population is aging. You'll decide which recurring analyses should become automated monitoring and work with software engineers to make that happen. You'll be the person the team turns to when someone asks "is this normal?" about the fleet. The ideal candidate is curious about how hardware fails and comfortable with messy data from many sources. They can turn "we're seeing something odd on a few satellites" into a rigorous analysis and a clear recommendation. Key job responsibilities As a Data Scientist, you will… - Analyze in-orbit telemetry to measure the health and performance of satellite subsystems, and detect anomalies and slow degradation early - Correlate in-orbit behavior with pre-launch data, including manufacturing, production test, and environmental test results, to find which ground measurements predict on-orbit performance. Feed those insights back into test limits and screening strategy - Correlate in-orbit data with what the ground segment sees (gateways, customer terminals) and with failure analysis of lab and returned units - Connect events across the fleet by build lot, component, configuration, firmware version, orbit, and operating conditions, so we can tell systemic issues apart from one-off events - Find patterns that point to hardware design weaknesses, measure their impact on the fleet, and present the evidence to hardware engineering teams with clear recommendations - Build visualizations and dashboards that support decisions in fleet health reviews, anomaly investigations, and design reviews - Work with software engineers to improve the data pipeline: define data requirements, improve data quality, link ground and in-orbit datasets, and automate recurring analyses - Own ambiguous investigations end to end, from forming the hypothesis to reporting the outcome to senior engineering leaders About the team Phased Array Antenna System Validation is the team that decides whether a satellite's phased array antennas (PAAs) are ready to fly. These are the electronically steered transmit and receive panels that form the link between the satellite and the ground. We're part of Amazon Leo, Amazon's low Earth orbit satellite constellation, and we own the test strategy, the automation, and the data pipeline that every PAA goes through on its way to orbit. That path runs from early component-level screening through full-panel functional testing and extended environmental stress testing across temperature, vacuum, and vibration. Our responsibility doesn't end at launch. We also own the analysis of telemetry from PAAs in orbit, and we use what the constellation tells us to improve how we test on the ground. We are a hardware-facing software and test team, and we sit where several disciplines meet. Our test automation directs firmware provisioning, self-tests, and environmental profiles on test stations at manufacturing sites. Over a single run it exercises thousands of measurement points: high-speed serial links, clocking, digital beamformer firmware, RF chains, power, and thermal behavior. A large part of our work is investigation. Over time we have built a growing catalog of failure signatures and their root causes, internal tools for analyzing long-duration tests, and AI-assisted agents that help turn raw logs into conclusions. We care about evidence over assumption. A label on a result isn't the result itself, and a search that finds nothing doesn't prove the problem has no history. Theories get tested against the data before they're accepted. We support multiple hardware generations at once, so the architecture, the questions, and the tooling keep changing. You'll work closely with antenna hardware, firmware, manufacturing, and reliability engineers who are glad to dig into a hard problem with you. Because we own both ends, from the first ground test to behavior in orbit, the loop is closed within the team. What we learn from the constellation goes straight back into our screens, thresholds, and test sequences, so that the next panel we validate is more likely to keep working in orbit.
