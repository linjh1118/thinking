Experimental support for RCDG1</a></li> </ul> </li><li data-toc-group data-astro-cid-ncn7dy3m> <div class="toc-section-row" data-astro-cid-ncn7dy3m> <a href="#references" data-astro-cid-ncn7dy3m>References</a>  </div>  </li> </ul> </div> </div> </nav> <script type="module" src="/_astro/TableOfContents.astro_astro_type_script_index_0_lang.CD3VP-20.js"></script> <div class="prose"><p>Proteins carry out much of the work inside cells. Understanding what they do is essential to uncovering disease mechanisms and developing new medicines. Yet the function of many proteins is still unknown, and the experiments that reveal it are slow. AI models that propose specific, testable hypotheses could point those experiments in the right direction.</p>
<p>BioReason-Pro<a class="cite" href="#ref-1" data-footnote-ref="" aria-label="Reference 1" data-astro-cid-jwlnubfr>[1]</a> aims to do this by fusing modalities: a) a protein model that encodes sequence and structure and b) text annotations from tools that predict protein functions and identify protein families and functional regions. An LLM uses these to reason in natural language about what the protein might do. The purpose of this multimodal architecture is to surface useful hypotheses about poorly understood proteins.</p>
<p>Generating these hypotheses beyond existing annotations depends on combining protein and text information effectively. In the original BioReason-Pro, text annotations supplied much of the information about functional regions the model learned to describe. We focused on understanding what the protein embeddings contributed to the model’s reasoning and how they shaped its hypotheses.</p>
<p>Building on the original release, we worked with NVIDIA and the Arc Institute to develop <strong>BioReason-Pro 2</strong>, which NVIDIA is releasing as a BioNeMo open model. During the model development process, our interpretability investigations revealed disproportionate reliance on text annotations and a scale mismatch between protein and text embeddings. These findings led us to <a href="#strengthening-bioreason-pros-understanding-of-proteins">revise the training inputs</a>, grounding its claims in specific protein regions and amino acids, and <a href="#making-protein-information-easier-to-process">rescale protein embeddings</a>. Together, these changes increased the share of annotated sequence features identified from 44% to 84% and improved localization to correctly predict 85% of the feature locations within five amino acids of their true positions.</p>
<p><strong>BioReason-Pro 2</strong> generated novel hypotheses about the function of two poorly characterized human proteins, <a href="#tracing-a-hypothesis-and-testing-it-in-the-lab">SMIM32</a> and <a href="#inspecting-a-hypothesis-of-a-protein-interaction">RCDG1</a><a class="cite bio-note" href="#note-rcdg1" data-footnote-ref aria-label="Note: original BioReason-Pro prediction">*</a>, which we validated in the wet lab. Because wet-lab experiments are slow and costly, deciding which hypotheses to test requires confidence in how the model arrived at them, so we also used interpretability to examine their basis. For SMIM32, interventions showed <a href="#smim32-circuit">how protein information supported the hypothesis</a> through intermediate reasoning steps, and for RCDG1, analysis of internal features revealed links between protein patterns and language in the model&#39;s explanations.</p>
<p>Fusion of biological modalities and language is a promising direction for building on specialized models to generate biological hypotheses. Understanding how each modality shapes the reasoning is therefore an important part of model development. Interpretability offers a way to guide improvements based on how models actually process and use biological information.</p>
<span id="strengthening-the-models-understanding-of-proteins"></span>
<h2 id="strengthening-bioreason-pros-understanding-of-proteins">Strengthening BioReason-Pro&#39;s understanding of proteins</h2>
<p>The original BioReason-Pro (BR-Pro) received protein information in several forms. A specialized model called ESM3<a class="cite" href="#ref-2" data-footnote-ref="" aria-label="Reference 2" data-astro-cid-jwlnubfr>[2]</a> converted sequence and available structure into numerical representations, or embeddings, for each amino acid residue, and a learned adapter<a class="cite" href="#ref-3" data-footnote-ref="" aria-label="Reference 3" data-astro-cid-jwlnubfr>[3]</a> made these accessible to the language model.</p>
<p>The model also received text that other tools generated from the same sequence. GO-GPT predicted Gene Ontology (GO)<a class="cite" href="#ref-4" data-footnote-ref="" aria-label="Reference 4" data-astro-cid-jwlnubfr>[4]</a> terms, a standard vocabulary for describing protein function, and InterPro<a class="cite" href="#ref-5" data-footnote-ref="" aria-label="Reference 5" data-astro-cid-jwlnubfr>[5]</a> (IPR) identified protein families, domains (regions with a characteristic structure or function), and sites.</p>
<p>ESM3 embeddings, GO-GPT predictions, and InterPro annotations are all derived from the same protein sequence. The original BR-Pro learned to describe proteins at the domain level, information InterPro already supplied as text. With InterPro and GO-GPT in its prompt, it could recover most of its GO term predictions without ESM3 embeddings, consistent with findings from an independent audit by Fang et al.<a class="cite" href="#ref-6" data-footnote-ref="" aria-label="Reference 6" data-astro-cid-jwlnubfr>[6]</a>. Further, making effective use of non-text inputs is a broader challenge: vision–language models can also perform worse on visual tasks than on their text counterparts (Weng et al.<a class="cite" href="#ref-7" data-footnote-ref="" aria-label="Reference 7" data-astro-cid-jwlnubfr>[7]</a>, Nikankin et al.<a class="cite" href="#ref-8" data-footnote-ref="" aria-label="Reference 8" data-astro-cid-jwlnubfr>[8]</a>).</p>
<p>To incentivize deeper use of the ESM3 embeddings, we made three changes (<a href="#figure-1">Figure 1</a>). First, we removed InterPro annotations from the training inputs and instead taught the model to identify them itself. Next, we enriched the training traces to ground the model&#39;s claims in specific domains, features, and even single amino acids. And finally, we rescaled the protein embeddings to the scale of the text in the LLM, here Qwen3.5-9B<a class="cite" href="#ref-9" data-footnote-ref="" aria-label="Reference 9" data-astro-cid-jwlnubfr>[9]</a>.</p>
<figure id="figure-1" class="figure figure-normal corners-none"> <img src="/_astro/model-changes.BHm8KgQY.svg" width="465.08" height="217" alt="Comparison of the BioReason-Pro and BioReason-Pro 2 architectures and inputs." loading="lazy" decoding="async"> <figcaption>Figure 1. Changes between BioReason-Pro and BioReason-Pro 2. (a) BioReason-Pro uses Qwen3-4B to read InterPro&#39;s domains and sites in its prompt. (b) BioReason-Pro 2 uses Qwen3.5-9B to identify domains and sequence features at the beginning of reasoning, and ESM3&#39;s embeddings are rescaled.</figcaption>  </figure>
<p>Furthermore, we expanded our evaluations beyond function prediction to measure how accurately the model grounds its claims in the protein. When the model discusses an amino acid at a particular position, was it actually there? Could it identify a sequence feature, such as a transmembrane helix or a signal peptide, and predict its location in the sequence? The final model showed substantial gains on these grounding checks, while also raising mean judge scores<a class="cite bio-note" href="#note-judge" data-footnote-ref aria-label="Note: five judging criteria">†</a> from 5.6 to 7.2 out of 10 and GO recall from 0.62 to 0.71 (<a href="#figure-2">Figure 2</a>).</p>
<figure id="figure-2" class="figure figure-normal corners-none"> <img src="/_astro/evaluation.k6iVNMhO.svg" width="518.74" height="212" alt="Function prediction and sequence grounding evaluations across four model variants." loading="lazy" decoding="async">  <figcaption>Figure 2. Evaluating function predictions and sequence grounding across four stages: BR-Pro (with IPR training), the original BR-Pro retrained on a Qwen3.5-9B backbone; BR-Pro (without IPR training); BR-Pro 2 (data change); and BR-Pro 2 (norm change). Grounding measures how accurately the answer describes the sequence, checking its cited amino acids, sequence features, and their locations against the protein&#39;s annotations. An LLM judge (Nemotron 3.5 Super VL<a class="cite" href="#ref-10" data-footnote-ref="" aria-label="Reference 10" data-astro-cid-jwlnubfr>[10]</a>) rates the function predictions against the protein&#39;s known biology, and GO recall shows the share of the protein&#39;s annotated GO terms that the answer recovers. All models are evaluated without InterPro in the prompt. N/A marks measures not applicable to BR-Pro (with IPR training), which was trained to read InterPro entries rather than to predict them.</figcaption> </figure>
<h3 id="enriching-what-the-model-learns">Enriching what the model learns</h3>
<p>In BioReason-Pro, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) was used to teach the model to write reasoning traces that, step by step, lead to functional predictions. These traces taught the model to link InterPro&#39;s domain annotations and GO-GPT&#39;s predictions into molecular hypotheses. To make the model more reliant on its protein embeddings, we changed both what the traces build on and how finely they describe the protein.</p>
<p>First, we removed InterPro annotations from the training inputs to create the BR-Pro (without IPR training) model, so identifying those annotations became part of the model&#39;s inference. Second, we trained BR-Pro 2 (data change) on over 230 thousand protein reasoning traces, twice as many as in the original corpus, with more granular reasoning that grounds the model&#39;s claims about domains, features, and even single amino acids in their positions in the sequence.</p>
<p>To compare changes in input reliance across models, we replayed the same reference trace through each model while blocking access to either the ESM3 embeddings or GO-GPT&#39;s predictions. For each word in the model’s reasoning, we measured how much the model&#39;s prediction changed when blocking ESM3 rather than GO-GPT. In the original BR-Pro, blocking either input had a similar effect on the model&#39;s token predictions, whereas in BR-Pro 2 (data change), ESM3 accounted for nearly all of the early change (<a href="#figure-3">Figure 3a</a>).</p>
<p>Compared with BR-Pro (without IPR training), BR-Pro 2 (data change) described the proteins in the test set at a much more granular level. The average number of features reported with a sequence location rose from 0.37 to 8.30 per protein. The model identified 83% of annotated features in the categories we evaluated, up from 44%, while the precision of its feature-presence claims remained similar, at 85% versus 87% (<a href="#figure-3">Figure 3b</a>).</p>
<p>We then removed the ESM3 embeddings from each model&#39;s input. Feature recall fell by 72% in the BR-Pro 2 (data change), compared with 21% in both the original BR-Pro and the BR-Pro (without IPR training) models, with nearly all of its located features disappearing (<a href="#figure-3">Figure 3c</a>). This shows that the gains in its feature grounding depend directly on the ESM3 embeddings.</p>
<figure id="figure-3" class="figure figure-normal corners-none"> <img src="/_astro/training-data.hLcQQv5i.svg" width="518.74" height="331" alt="Effects of revised training on protein input reliance, feature coverage, and grounding." loading="lazy" decoding="async"> <figcaption>Figure 3. Improved training elicits more protein detail and greater dependence on the embeddings. Panel (a) illustrates input effects on a reference trace replayed through each model; (b) measures feature coverage and accuracy; (c) measures changes after removing the protein. For the 9B models, (c) uses separate intact reruns, so its changes cannot be subtracted directly from b. Feature-presence changes use 1,000 proteins; other aggregate measures use approximately 6,000. Error bars are 95% bootstrap intervals. N/A marks measures not applicable to BR-Pro, as in Figure 2.</figcaption>  </figure>
<p>BR-Pro 2 (data change) described far more features, but often placed them incorrectly. Only 57% of its predicted locations were within five amino acids of the feature&#39;s true position. We next examined what limited that precision.</p>
<h3 id="making-protein-information-easier-to-process">Making protein information easier to process</h3>
<p>In the BR-Pro 2 (data change), we found a large mismatch between the numerical scale of the protein and text representations (<a href="#figure-4">Figure 4a</a>). Each layer adds an update to the representation it receives. In vision–language models, Li et al.<a class="cite" href="#ref-11" data-footnote-ref="" aria-label="Reference 11" data-astro-cid-jwlnubfr>[11]</a> show that a large representation for the vision inputs can lead to much smaller updates in comparison to text.</p>
<p>We tested the contribution of these updates by resetting the protein representations to their input values before each layer, an intervention called freezing<a class="cite" href="#ref-12" data-footnote-ref="" aria-label="Reference 12" data-astro-cid-jwlnubfr>[12]</a>. Text positions could still read the protein information, but updates could not accumulate at protein positions. Freezing had relatively modest effects in the BR-Pro 2 (data change) (<a href="#figure-4">Figure 4c</a>): the model used the protein input, but appeared to benefit little from updating those representations across layers.</p>
<p>We first applied LayerNorm<a class="cite" href="#ref-13" data-footnote-ref="" aria-label="Reference 13" data-astro-cid-jwlnubfr>[13]</a> to reduce the scale mismatch. Although it brought the protein representations closer to the text scale, it also removed the variation in magnitude across amino acid embeddings. This variant often misreported sequence lengths and placed features beyond the protein&#39;s end.</p>
<p>We then applied a block RMS norm<a class="cite" href="#ref-14" data-footnote-ref="" aria-label="Reference 14" data-astro-cid-jwlnubfr>[14]</a>, which divides all of a protein&#39;s representations by one shared factor, bringing the whole protein down to the text&#39;s scale at once while preserving the differences between them. Compared with BR-Pro 2 (data change), the resulting BR-Pro 2 (norm change) raised the share of cited amino acids matching their stated positions from 15.1% to 95.5%, and feature-location precision within five amino acids from 57.0% to 84.5% (<a href="#figure-4">Figure 4b</a>).</p>
<p>This time, freezing protein representations caused much larger losses in the BR-Pro 2 (norm change) model (<a href="#figure-4">Figure 4c</a>). Together, these results show that the norm change enabled more useful processing of protein information.</p>
<figure id="figure-4" class="figure figure-normal corners-none"> <img src="/_astro/normalization.Cm_X6zB6.svg" width="518.74" height="186" alt="Representation magnitudes, grounding precision, and the effects of freezing protein representations." loading="lazy" decoding="async"> <figcaption>Figure 4. Normalization improves sequence descriptions and makes updates at protein positions more consequential. Panel a shows representation magnitudes; b shows prediction precision; c shows the effect of freezing protein representations. Panels a and c use 256 proteins; b uses the full test set. Panel c uses each model’s intact baseline from that separate evaluation. Error bars are 95% bootstrap intervals.</figcaption>  </figure>
<span id="tracing-protein-information-into-an-answer"></span>
<h2 id="tracing-each-inputs-influence-on-the-answer">Tracing each input&#39;s influence on the answer</h2>
<p>BioReason-Pro 2 reads several inputs at once, so we asked which parts of its explanation each one influences. We replayed saved responses while blocking access to one input at a time, extending the intervention approach in Thought Anchors<a class="cite" href="#ref-15" data-footnote-ref="" aria-label="Reference 15" data-astro-cid-jwlnubfr>[15]</a>.</p>
<p>ESM3&#39;s effects concentrated on sequence lengths, amino-acid positions and identities, and domain descriptions. GO-GPT&#39;s effects concentrated on biological processes and cellular locations. Optionally supplied interaction-partner information disproportionately affected partner names and interaction statements. These comparisons are relative to each input&#39;s average effect (<a href="#figure-5">Figure 5</a>).</p>
<figure id="figure-5" class="figure figure-normal corners-none"> <img src="/_astro/input-influence.jZB28ECx.svg" width="518.74" height="249" alt="Enrichment and depletion of input effects across claim categories." loading="lazy" decoding="async"> <figcaption>Figure 5. Different inputs (rows) influence different parts of the explanation (columns). Circle size shows how strongly effects concentrate on each claim category relative to that input’s average effect. Filled and open circles mark enrichment and depletion supported by 95% bootstrap intervals. These are relative effects, not percentages of information contributed by each source.</figcaption>  </figure>
<p>The contributions overlap. For example, ESM3, GO-GPT, and the prompt template can all affect parts of a sequence description. This analysis shows where inputs matter; it does not assign every claim a single source or establish that the claim is correct.</p>
<p>We also tested newly generated answers after removing the ESM3 protein embeddings, replacing them with those of another protein of similar length, or shuffling the sequence before computing its embeddings. The other inputs stayed fixed, and we evaluated answers against the original protein embeddings.</p>
<figure id="figure-6" class="figure figure-normal corners-none"> <img src="/_astro/input-interventions.B9CyfM-l.svg" width="518.74" height="128" alt="Judge score and GO recall changes after removing, replacing, or shuffling protein embeddings." loading="lazy" decoding="async"> <figcaption>Figure 6. BioReason-Pro 2’s answers are much more sensitive to ESM3 embedding removal, replacement, and shuffling. Changes are measured against each model’s unperturbed run, without InterPro annotations in the prompt. Judge scores use 1,000 proteins and GO recall approximately 6,000; error bars are 95% bootstrap intervals.</figcaption>  </figure>
<p>The original BR-Pro&#39;s judge scores and GO recall changed the least. BR-Pro (without IPR training) showed substantial losses, and both BR-Pro 2 models showed larger losses still (<a href="#figure-6">Figure 6</a>). This supports a greater reliance on the ESM3 embeddings in the later models. We next examined that dependence in a specific biological hypothesis.</p>
<span id="following-a-hypothesis-from-protein-to-experiment"></span>
<h2 id="tracing-a-hypothesis-and-testing-it-in-the-lab">Tracing a hypothesis and testing it in the lab</h2>
<p>SMIM32 is a small human protein with 103 amino acids. Its single cysteine, Cys75, lies at the end of a predicted membrane-spanning helix. BioReason-Pro 2 proposed that a lipid could attach to this residue through a modification called S-acylation. The model’s explanation connected the cysteine’s position on the side facing the cell’s interior to the proposed modification and a possible effect on the protein’s tail.</p>
<p>The input included written predictions about how the protein sits in the membrane, but did not identify cysteine 75. We wanted to test how the protein embeddings and the intermediate reasoning traces contributed to the S-acylation proposal.</p>
<h3 id="changing-the-protein-changes-the-hypothesis">Changing the protein changes the hypothesis</h3>
<p>With the unmodified protein, the model proposed Cys75 S-acylation in 31% of 300 sampled responses. Removing the protein input produced no proposals in 300 responses. Replacing Cys75 with serine (C75S) also eliminated the proposal in 300 responses, even though the model still received embeddings for the modified protein (<a href="#figure-7">Figure 7</a>).</p>
<p>We then moved the cysteine to test whether the proposal depended on its position. Moving it nearby, to position 78, reduced the S-acylation proposal rate to 13%. At position 20, the model identified the relocated cysteine in 24.3% of responses but proposed its S-acylation in only 2.7% (<a href="#figure-7">Figure 7</a>). The model was able to separate finding a cysteine from proposing a reaction at that location.</p>
<figure id="figure-7" class="figure figure-normal corners-none"> <img src="/_astro/cysteine-interventions.BoXODpp3.svg" width="368.5" height="145" alt="Modification proposals and cysteine identification under deletion, mutation, and relocation." loading="lazy" decoding="async"> <figcaption>Figure 7. The modification proposal responds to the cysteine&#39;s presence and position. Each condition has 300 samples, the same in both panels; error bars are 95% Wilson intervals. Zero means no occurrences in those samples. The relocations are C75S+R78C and C75S+L20C. At position 20, 73 of 300 continuations name Cys20 and 8 propose its S-acylation.</figcaption>  </figure>
<span id="testing-the-intermediate-reasoning"></span>
<h3 id="tracing-the-depth-of-influence-of-protein-information">Tracing the depth of influence of protein information</h3>
<p>We next tested whether intermediate reasoning steps carried protein information to the proposal and whether earlier reasoning helped the model identify relevant protein details. We supplied selected sentences from a saved response and repeatedly sampled continuations, varying both the supplied text and the protein input. We measured how these interventions changed the frequency of the modification proposal.</p>
<p><a href="#smim32-circuit">Figure 8a</a> summarizes the tested influences as a reasoning influence circuit showing several ways protein information can affect the proposal: through the residue description, through earlier context, and with preceding text held fixed.</p>
<p>Supplying the sentence locating Cys75 at the end of the membrane helix raised the rate of proposals anywhere in the continuation from 31.0% to 65.7%. With the C75S mutation, the same sentence raised it from zero to 63.7% (<a href="#figure-8">Figure 8b</a>). The model could therefore use the written description of Cys75 to produce the hypothesis, as a “sufficient” replacement of the protein input.</p>
<p>We also tested whether protein information affected the proposal when the preceding reasoning was held fixed. With all ten sentences prefilled, changing the protein to C75S reduced the rate of S-acylation proposals in the next sentence from 33.0% to 13.3% (<a href="#figure-8">Figure 8c</a>). In this context, the protein input continued to influence the proposal despite the unchanged reasoning text.</p>
<p>An earlier sentence describing how the protein sits in the membrane helped the model identify Cys75. Including this sentence increased the rate of locating Cys75 in the next sentence from 5.7% to 29.3%. Neither condition produced that description with C75S (<a href="#figure-8">Figure 8d</a>). Here, the earlier reasoning helped the model identify a residue present in the protein.</p>

<span id="testing-the-prediction-in-the-laboratory"></span>
<h3 id="validating-the-prediction-in-the-laboratory">Validating the prediction in the laboratory</h3>
<p>Laboratory experiments confirmed Cys75-dependent S-acylation under the tested conditions. A modification-associated signal appeared in the original protein and disappeared when cysteine 75 was replaced with alanine (<a href="#supplementary-figure-1-experimental-support-for-smim32">Supplementary Figure 1</a>). This supports cysteine 75 as the attachment site, while leaving the attached fatty acid, responsible enzyme, and proposed effect on the protein’s tail unresolved.</p>
<p>The model’s explanation also contained an implausible assignment of a signal peptide, a sequence that directs a protein into a cellular transport pathway, and an incorrect protein-family identity. However, the model also proposed S-acylation in responses that rejected the signal-peptide assignment (<a href="#supplementary-figure-2-modification-proposals-across-signal-peptide-explanations">Supplementary Figure 2</a>).</p>
<p>A confirmed prediction can emerge from an explanation that mixes useful observations with faulty intermediate steps. Interpretability helps reveal that mixture: the model interventions test what the prediction relies on, while the laboratory experiment tests the corresponding biology.</p>
<h2 id="finding-shared-features-in-proteins-and-language">Finding shared features in proteins and language</h2>
<p>The interventions above test how inputs affect outputs. We also examined the patterns represented inside the model, looking for features that respond both to a protein region and to the model’s discussion of it.</p>
<p>We used sparse autoencoders<a class="cite" href="#ref-16" data-footnote-ref="" aria-label="Reference 16" data-astro-cid-jwlnubfr>[16]</a>, or SAEs, which separate model activity into features that can be inspected at individual protein and text positions. Across the same 418 proteins and each model’s own generated explanations, the BR-Pro 2 (data change) had more features showing shared protein–language activity, with the largest count in the BR-Pro 2 (norm change) (<a href="#figure-9">Figure 9</a>).</p>

<h3 id="distinguishing-a-protein-fold-from-a-membrane-anchor">Distinguishing a protein fold from a membrane anchor</h3>
<p>A water-repelling stretch of amino acids can sit inside a membrane, but it can also be buried inside a folded protein. For LRRC74A, the model received a prediction from the sequence-analysis tool Phobius<a class="cite" href="#ref-17" data-footnote-ref="" aria-label="Reference 17" data-astro-cid-jwlnubfr>[17]</a> that one stretch crossed a membrane. Its explanation instead placed that stretch inside a horseshoe-shaped structure formed by leucine-rich repeats, or LRRs.</p>
<p>One SAE feature responded at selected repeat positions and to words such as “horseshoe” and “buried hydrophobic” in the model’s explanation. In SERK4, which contains both repeats and a separate membrane anchor, activity concentrated in the illustrated repeat region (<a href="#sae-lrr-structures">Figure 10a</a>).</p>
<p>We then fixed the feature and scoring rule and tested a separately selected panel outside the model’s fine-tuning split. All 20 LRR proteins scored above all 27 controls (<a href="#sae-lrr-structures">Figure 10b</a>). This supports the feature’s selectivity on that panel, while leaving open whether the model causally uses it to resolve the competing structural descriptions.</p>

<h3 id="inspecting-a-hypothesis-of-a-protein-interaction">Inspecting a hypothesis of a protein interaction</h3>
<p>RCDG1 is a poorly understood protein associated with kidney cancer<a class="cite" href="#ref-18" data-footnote-ref="" aria-label="Reference 18" data-astro-cid-jwlnubfr>[18]</a>. BioReason-Pro 2 proposed an association with RAB5A, a protein involved in transporting material within cells.</p>
<p>Two SAE features highlighted complementary parts of the explanation: language about GTPases, a class of molecular switches that includes RAB5A, and language about cellular trafficking (<a href="#sae-rab-structures">Figure 11a-b</a>). Both features are active in the same passage for RCDG1: R3266 activates more strongly on the Rab5 text while R23652 activates on trafficking-related wording. Tests on additional proteins and passages supported these interpretations, although the trafficking-related feature was less selective (<a href="#sae-rab-structures">Figure 11c</a>).</p>
<figure class="figure structure-figure corners-none" id="sae-rab-structures" data-astro-cid-qibtyfhs> <div class="structure-image" data-astro-cid-qibtyfhs> <img src="/_astro/rcdg1-features.DBdO23pI.svg" width="2048" height="825" alt="GTPase and transport feature activity in proteins and the RCDG1 explanation." loading="lazy" decoding="async" data-astro-cid-qibtyfhs="true"> <button type="button" class="structure-hotspot" data-structure-open="P25228" aria-label="Explore Rab-3 structure in 3D" aria-haspopup="dialog" aria-controls="sae-structure-explorer" style="left:0.732421875%;top:7.878787878787878%;width:35.888671875%;height:51.515151515151516%" data-astro-cid-qibtyfhs><span data-astro-cid-qibtyfhs>Explore in 3D ↗</span></button> </div>  <figcaption data-astro-cid-qibtyfhs>Figure 11. Features associated with GTPases and cellular trafficking highlight parts of the RCDG1 explanation. Panel a shows a Rab-3 reference protein; b shows feature activity in the RCDG1 text. Panel c tests selectivity across 39 proteins and 78 sentences. The sentence sample comes from six proteins, limiting its independent coverage. These scores assess feature associations, not RCDG1 binding accuracy.</figcaption> </figure>
<p>We validated the association in the wet lab using co-immunoprecipitation experiments<a class="cite" href="#ref-1" data-footnote-ref="" aria-label="Reference 1" data-astro-cid-jwlnubfr>[1]</a>, which test whether proteins are recovered together (<a href="#supplementary-figure-3-experimental-support-for-rcdg1">Supplementary Figure 3</a>).</p>
<p>This gives a poorly understood cancer biomarker a new, specific, experimentally supported partner and points toward a more central role in cellular trafficking. RCDG1 was among the model&#39;s training proteins, yet its training trace mentioned neither this interaction nor a role in trafficking, showing that the model can propose new hypotheses even for proteins it has seen.</p>
<p>These features provide a way to inspect the concepts associated with an explanation. Their activity does not establish the mechanism behind the predicted interaction. As with LRRs, they offer candidates for further investigation.</p>
<h2 id="sampling-hypotheses-from-the-model">Sampling hypotheses from the model</h2>
<p>BioReason-Pro 2 is a hypothesis generator. Repeated generations for a protein<a class="cite bio-note" href="#note-sampling" data-footnote-ref aria-label="Note: sampling settings">‡</a> offer a diversity of explanations, and the best of ten raises the mean judge score from 6.8 to 8.4 and GO recall from 0.70 to 0.82, while reliability more than doubles (<a href="#figure-12">Figure 12</a>). Used as a reinforcement learning (RL) reward, LLM-as-a-judge raised a single generation&#39;s judge score from 6.8 to 7.6. Used as a reviewer, a frontier LLM can compare a dozen generations and distill the most plausible novel hypotheses for scientists to test. To that end, we are open-sourcing an atlas of 33.5 million BioReason-Pro 2 reasoning traces across over 300,000 proteins.</p>
<p><a class="bio-hypotheses-link" href="/research/building-stronger-biological-reasoning-models/hypotheses">Explore the model’s hypotheses</a>: choose a protein, follow alternative reasoning paths, and compare the model’s proposals with native or shuffled protein inputs.</p>

Best of k samples per protein. (a) Mean judge score and (b) judge reliability, on 1,000 proteins. (c) GO recall on approximately 6,000 test proteins.</figcaption>  </figure>
<span id="building-models-we-can-examine-and-improve"></span>
<h2 id="towards-building-models-we-can-examine-and-improve">Towards building models we can examine and improve</h2>
<p>Interpretability guided both how we built BioReason-Pro 2 and how we tested its hypotheses. Input interventions exposed coarse use of the ESM3 embeddings, which led us to remove InterPro from training and enrich the granularity of protein grounding in the traces. Internal representations also revealed a scale mismatch between protein and text norms, which we fixed by appropriate rescaling.</p>
<p>For SMIM32, we tested how protein information influenced a hypothesis at several levels: directly on the modification proposal, through the description of the cysteine, and through earlier reasoning about membrane topology. For RCDG1, SAE features showed which concepts its explanation drew on. Both hypotheses were then validated in the wet lab.</p>
<p>This kind of analysis matters most for models that combine different kinds of input. Protein embeddings and text annotations can supply overlapping evidence, and each can make different mistakes. Evaluating only the final answer can miss how each input contributes, and where a change in training would help.</p>
<p>There is further work to do: BioReason-Pro 2 was trained for a particular format for discussing protein function and struggles to follow requests for other formats; interleaving question-answering traces may help broaden that flexibility. Recent results from Tu et al.<a class="cite" href="#ref-19" data-footnote-ref="" aria-label="Reference 19" data-astro-cid-jwlnubfr>[19]</a>, showing strong next-amino-acid prediction by Fable 5.2 on little-studied proteins, also motivate evaluating how frontier language models compare on biological hypothesis generation. We plan to include this comparison in our upcoming preprint.</p>
<p>Our work demonstrates how interpretability can complement end-to-end benchmarks by revealing how models use their biological inputs, identifying limitations that performance metrics alone may miss, and guiding systematic improvements to multimodal biological reasoning models.</p>
<p>If you are building models for biology or other scientific domains, we’d like to work with you to understand how they use their inputs, develop evaluations that reflect the scientific tasks you care about, and use those findings to guide model improvements.</p>
