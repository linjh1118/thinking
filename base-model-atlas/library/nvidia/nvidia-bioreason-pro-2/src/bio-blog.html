Proteins carry out much of the work inside cells. Understanding what they do is essential to uncovering disease mechanisms and developing new medicines. Yet the function of many proteins is still unknown, and the experiments that reveal it are slow. AI models that propose specific, testable hypotheses could point those experiments in the right direction. 
BioReason-Pro [1] aims to do this by fusing modalities: a) a protein model that encodes sequence and structure and b) text annotations from tools that predict protein functions and identify protein families and functional regions. An LLM uses these to reason in natural language about what the protein might do. The purpose of this multimodal architecture is to surface useful hypotheses about poorly understood proteins. 
Generating these hypotheses beyond existing annotations depends on combining protein and text information effectively. In the original BioReason-Pro, text annotations supplied much of the information about functional regions the model learned to describe. We focused on understanding what the protein embeddings contributed to the model’s reasoning and how they shaped its hypotheses. 
Building on the original release, we worked with NVIDIA and the Arc Institute to develop BioReason-Pro 2 , which NVIDIA is releasing as a BioNeMo open model. During the model development process, our interpretability investigations revealed disproportionate reliance on text annotations and a scale mismatch between protein and text embeddings. These findings led us to revise the training inputs , grounding its claims in specific protein regions and amino acids, and rescale protein embeddings . Together, these changes increased the share of annotated sequence features identified from 44% to 84% and improved localization to correctly predict 85% of the feature locations within five amino acids of their true positions. 
BioReason-Pro 2 generated novel hypotheses about the function of two poorly characterized human proteins, SMIM32 and RCDG1 * , which we validated in the wet lab. Because wet-lab experiments are slow and costly, deciding which hypotheses to test requires confidence in how the model arrived at them, so we also used interpretability to examine their basis. For SMIM32, interventions showed how protein information supported the hypothesis through intermediate reasoning steps, and for RCDG1, analysis of internal features revealed links between protein patterns and language in the model's explanations. 
Fusion of biological modalities and language is a promising direction for building on specialized models to generate biological hypotheses. Understanding how each modality shapes the reasoning is therefore an important part of model development. Interpretability offers a way to guide improvements based on how models actually process and use biological information. 
Strengthening BioReason-Pro's understanding of proteins 
The original BioReason-Pro (BR-Pro) received protein information in several forms. A specialized model called ESM3 [2] converted sequence and available structure into numerical representations, or embeddings, for each amino acid residue, and a learned adapter [3] made these accessible to the language model. 
The model also received text that other tools generated from the same sequence. GO-GPT predicted Gene Ontology (GO) [4] terms, a standard vocabulary for describing protein function, and InterPro [5] (IPR) identified protein families, domains (regions with a characteristic structure or function), and sites. 
ESM3 embeddings, GO-GPT predictions, and InterPro annotations are all derived from the same protein sequence. The original BR-Pro learned to describe proteins at the domain level, information InterPro already supplied as text. With InterPro and GO-GPT in its prompt, it could recover most of its GO term predictions without ESM3 embeddings, consistent with findings from an independent audit by Fang et al. [6] . Further, making effective use of non-text inputs is a broader challenge: vision–language models can also perform worse on visual tasks than on their text counterparts (Weng et al. [7] , Nikankin et al. [8] ). 
To incentivize deeper use of the ESM3 embeddings, we made three changes ( Figure 1 ). First, we removed InterPro annotations from the training inputs and instead taught the model to identify them itself. Next, we enriched the training traces to ground the model's claims in specific domains, features, and even single amino acids. And finally, we rescaled the protein embeddings to the scale of the text in the LLM, here Qwen3.5-9B [9] . Figure 1. Changes between BioReason-Pro and BioReason-Pro 2. (a) BioReason-Pro uses Qwen3-4B to read InterPro's domains and sites in its prompt. (b) BioReason-Pro 2 uses Qwen3.5-9B to identify domains and sequence features at the beginning of reasoning, and ESM3's embeddings are rescaled. 
Furthermore, we expanded our evaluations beyond function prediction to measure how accurately the model grounds its claims in the protein. When the model discusses an amino acid at a particular position, was it actually there? Could it identify a sequence feature, such as a transmembrane helix or a signal peptide, and predict its location in the sequence? The final model showed substantial gains on these grounding checks, while also raising mean judge scores † from 5.6 to 7.2 out of 10 and GO recall from 0.62 to 0.71 ( Figure 2 ). Figure 2. Evaluating function predictions and sequence grounding across four stages: BR-Pro (with IPR training), the original BR-Pro retrained on a Qwen3.5-9B backbone; BR-Pro (without IPR training); BR-Pro 2 (data change); and BR-Pro 2 (norm change). Grounding measures how accurately the answer describes the sequence, checking its cited amino acids, sequence features, and their locations against the protein's annotations. An LLM judge (Nemotron 3.5 Super VL [10] ) rates the function predictions against the protein's known biology, and GO recall shows the share of the protein's annotated GO terms that the answer recovers. All models are evaluated without InterPro in the prompt. N/A marks measures not applicable to BR-Pro (with IPR training), which was trained to read InterPro entries rather than to predict them. 
Enriching what the model learns 
In BioReason-Pro, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) was used to teach the model to write reasoning traces that, step by step, lead to functional predictions. These traces taught the model to link InterPro's domain annotations and GO-GPT's predictions into molecular hypotheses. To make the model more reliant on its protein embeddings, we changed both what the traces build on and how finely they describe the protein. 
First, we removed InterPro annotations from the training inputs to create the BR-Pro (without IPR training) model, so identifying those annotations became part of the model's inference. Second, we trained BR-Pro 2 (data change) on over 230 thousand protein reasoning traces, twice as many as in the original corpus, with more granular reasoning that grounds the model's claims about domains, features, and even single amino acids in their positions in the sequence. 
To compare changes in input reliance across models, we replayed the same reference trace through each model while blocking access to either the ESM3 embeddings or GO-GPT's predictions. For each word in the model’s reasoning, we measured how much the model's prediction changed when blocking ESM3 rather than GO-GPT. In the original BR-Pro, blocking either input had a similar effect on the model's token predictions, whereas in BR-Pro 2 (data change), ESM3 accounted for nearly all of the early change ( Figure 3a ). 
Compared with BR-Pro (without IPR training), BR-Pro 2 (data change) described the proteins in the test set at a much more granular level. The average number of features reported with a sequence location rose from 0.37 to 8.30 per protein. The model identified 83% of annotated features in the categories we evaluated, up from 44%, while the precision of its feature-presence claims remained similar, at 85% versus 87% ( Figure 3b ). 
We then removed the ESM3 embeddings from each model's input. Feature recall fell by 72% in the BR-Pro 2 (data change), compared with 21% in both the original BR-Pro and the BR-Pro (without IPR training) models, with nearly all of its located features disappearing ( Figure 3c ). This shows that the gains in its feature grounding depend directly on the ESM3 embeddings. Figure 3. Improved training elicits more protein detail and greater dependence on the embeddings. BR-Pro 2 (data change) described far more features, but often placed them incorrectly. Only 57% of its predicted locations were within five amino acids of the feature's true position. We next examined what limited that precision. 
Making protein information easier to process 
In the BR-Pro 2 (data change), we found a large mismatch between the numerical scale of the protein and text representations ( Figure 4a ). Each layer adds an update to the representation it receives. In vision–language models, Li et al. [11] show that a large representation for the vision inputs can lead to much smaller updates in comparison to text. 
We tested the contribution of these updates by resetting the protein representations to their input values before each layer, an intervention called freezing [12] . Text positions could still read the protein information, but updates could not accumulate at protein positions. Freezing had relatively modest effects in the BR-Pro 2 (data change) ( Figure 4c ): the model used the protein input, but appeared to benefit little from updating those representations across layers. 
We first applied LayerNorm [13] to reduce the scale mismatch. Although it brought the protein representations closer to the text scale, it also removed the variation in magnitude across amino acid embeddings. This variant often misreported sequence lengths and placed features beyond the protein's end. 
We then applied a block RMS norm [14] , which divides all of a protein's representations by one shared factor, bringing the whole protein down to the text's scale at once while preserving the differences between them. Compared with BR-Pro 2 (data change), the resulting BR-Pro 2 (norm change) raised the share of cited amino acids matching their stated positions from 15.1% to 95.5%, and feature-location precision within five amino acids from 57.0% to 84.5% ( Figure 4b ). 
This time, freezing protein representations caused much larger losses in the BR-Pro 2 (norm change) model ( Figure 4c ). Together, these results show that the norm change enabled more useful processing of protein information. Figure 4. Tracing each input's influence on the answer 
BioReason-Pro 2 reads several inputs at once, so we asked which parts of its explanation each one influences. We replayed saved responses while blocking access to one input at a time, extending the intervention approach in Thought Anchors [15] . 
ESM3's effects concentrated on sequence lengths, amino-acid positions and identities, and domain descriptions. GO-GPT's effects concentrated on biological processes and cellular locations. Optionally supplied interaction-partner information disproportionately affected partner names and interaction statements. These comparisons are relative to each input's average effect ( Figure 5 ). Figure 5. Different inputs (rows) influence different parts of the explanation (columns). Circle size shows how strongly effects concentrate on each claim category relative to that input’s average effect. Filled and open circles mark enrichment and depletion supported by 95% bootstrap intervals. These are relative effects, not percentages of information contributed by each source. 
The contributions overlap. For example, ESM3, GO-GPT, and the prompt template can all affect parts of a sequence description. This analysis shows where inputs matter; it does not assign every claim a single source or establish that the claim is correct. 
We also tested newly generated answers after removing the ESM3 protein embeddings, replacing them with those of another protein of similar length, or shuffling the sequence before computing its embeddings. The other inputs stayed fixed, and we evaluated answers against the original protein embeddings. Figure 6. The original BR-Pro's judge scores and GO recall changed the least. BR-Pro (without IPR training) showed substantial losses, and both BR-Pro 2 models showed larger losses still ( Figure 6 ). This supports a greater reliance on the ESM3 embeddings in the later models. We next examined that dependence in a specific biological hypothesis. 
Tracing a hypothesis and testing it in the lab 
SMIM32 is a small human protein with 103 amino acids. Its single cysteine, Cys75, lies at the end of a predicted membrane-spanning helix. BioReason-Pro 2 proposed that a lipid could attach to this residue through a modification called S-acylation. The model’s explanation connected the cysteine’s position on the side facing the cell’s interior to the proposed modification and a possible effect on the protein’s tail. 
The input included written predictions about how the protein sits in the membrane, but did not identify cysteine 75. We wanted to test how the protein embeddings and the intermediate reasoning traces contributed to the S-acylation proposal. 
Changing the protein changes the hypothesis 
With the unmodified protein, the model proposed Cys75 S-acylation in 31% of 300 sampled responses. Removing the protein input produced no proposals in 300 responses. Replacing Cys75 with serine (C75S) also eliminated the proposal in 300 responses, even though the model still received embeddings for the modified protein ( Figure 7 ). 
We then moved the cysteine to test whether the proposal depended on its position. Moving it nearby, to position 78, reduced the S-acylation proposal rate to 13%. At position 20, the model identified the relocated cysteine in 24.3% of responses but proposed its S-acylation in only 2.7% ( Figure 7 ). The model was able to separate finding a cysteine from proposing a reaction at that location. Figure 7. The modification proposal responds to the cysteine's presence and position. Each condition has 300 samples, the same in both panels; error bars are 95% Wilson intervals. Zero means no occurrences in those samples. The relocations are C75S+R78C and C75S+L20C. At position 20, 73 of 300 continuations name Cys20 and 8 propose its S-acylation. 
Tracing the depth of influence of protein information 
We next tested whether intermediate reasoning steps carried protein information to the proposal and whether earlier reasoning helped the model identify relevant protein details. We supplied selected sentences from a saved response and repeatedly sampled continuations, varying both the supplied text and the protein input. We measured how these interventions changed the frequency of the modification proposal. 
Figure 8a summarizes the tested influences as a reasoning influence circuit showing several ways protein information can affect the proposal: through the residue description, through earlier context, and with preceding text held fixed. 
Supplying the sentence locating Cys75 at the end of the membrane helix raised the rate of proposals anywhere in the continuation from 31.0% to 65.7%. With the C75S mutation, the same sentence raised it from zero to 63.7% ( Figure 8b ). The model could therefore use the written description of Cys75 to produce the hypothesis, as a “sufficient” replacement of the protein input. 
We also tested whether protein information affected the proposal when the preceding reasoning was held fixed. With all ten sentences prefilled, changing the protein to C75S reduced the rate of S-acylation proposals in the next sentence from 33.0% to 13.3% ( Figure 8c ). In this context, the protein input continued to influence the proposal despite the unchanged reasoning text. 
An earlier sentence describing how the protein sits in the membrane helped the model identify Cys75. Including this sentence increased the rate of locating Cys75 in the next sentence from 5.7% to 29.3%. Neither condition produced that description with C75S ( Figure 8d ). The basic tail itself, twenty-eight residues with net positive charge, is the kind of surface that binds a partner's acidic face or an acidic phospholipid headgroup, and either way it is a binding surface, not a catalyst. So the molecular activity I can defend from the chain alone is protein binding at a membrane, executed by a short basic tail behind one helix, with no chemistry of its own anywhere on the sequence. 
What that binding serves depends on what the tail can reach, and the family assignment tells me the neighbourhood: gamma subunits are auxiliary subunits of voltage-gated calcium channels, small single-pass proteins that pack against the alpha-1 pore and shift its voltage dependence, its current amplitude and its kinetics without conducting anything themselves. This chain carries no second helix, no pore and no voltage sensor, so whatever it does to a channel it does as a regulator from the side, which is channel regulator activity rather than channel activity. The difference from the canonical gammas is the signal peptide: those proteins stay membrane-bound and modulate gating from within the complex, but a chain cut loose at the endoplasmic reticulum cannot remain in it, so this one either travels with the channel to the surface and modulates from there or acts at a distance after secretion. The calcium dependence in its name points the same way, since a secreted protein whose activity tracks extracellular calcium is one that senses the ion it travels with, and the histidine at 79 with the acidic residues scattered through the shed region are the only candidates for that chemistry on the chain, though a single histidine is weak evidence and I hold it lightly. The potassium channel side of its name is harder still, since nothing here resembles a potassium-selective filter or a voltage sensor, so if it touches potassium channels at all it does so as another auxiliary, which is the same binding activity on a different partner. Either way the process is the modulation of cation transport across the plasma membrane by a subunit that never conducts, and the compartment is the cell surface where the channels it modulates sit. 
The mechanism, then, is association rather than catalysis. A single-pass helix with a basic tail is a classic auxiliary-subunit architecture: the helix packs laterally against the transmembrane bundle of a pore-forming channel, the tail reaches the cytosolic loops or the acidic inner leaflet to stabilise the association, and the cysteine at 75 locks the geometry by palmitoylation. For a voltage-gated calcium channel the partners are the alpha-1 pore itself, the alpha-2-delta and beta auxiliary subunits that complete the complex, and the calmodulin that reads the calcium the channel passes; for a voltage-gated potassium channel they are the Kv pore-forming subunits with their own auxiliary proteins. The signal peptide adds a second, independent mechanism: once cleaved, the fifty-one-residue piece is small enough to diffuse, and if it retains any affinity for its partners it could act as a soluble competitor, a shed regulator that tunes channel number at the surface by binding extracellular loops or the same sites the membrane-bound gamma would occupy, which would make it an antagonist or a scavenger rather than an auxiliary. That reading is extrapolation from the architecture, not something the chain proves, and I flag it as such. What the chain does prove is a protein that binds, at a membrane, to something it modulates, and that the binding is basic-tail and helix-mediated, with the shed form the piece left over after the signal is cut. 
Because the chain is short and single-pass, the interaction partners are few and obligate rather than a signalling cascade: the Sec61 translocon and the signal peptidase complex for its entry and cleavage, a ZDHHC-family palmitoyltransferase for the cysteine at 75, and then the channel it modulates, whose identity the family name and the organism's biology both point to as a voltage-gated calcium or potassium channel at the cell surface. I cannot name the specific alpha-1 or Kv subunit from the chain, so I name the class instead, and I add calmodulin as a plausible downstream reader of the calcium the modulated channel passes, since a regulator of a calcium channel that never touches calmodulin would be ignoring the universal sensor of that current. Losing the protein would not abolish conduction, since it carries no pore, so the phenotype would be a shift in channel number, gating or surface density rather than silence, which fits an auxiliary subunit and a shed regulator alike. Final answer - Summary: A short type I single-pass membrane protein of the voltage-gated cation channel auxiliary family, synthesized with a cleavable signal peptide that routes it through the endoplasmic reticulum to the plasma membrane and is then cut away, leaving a shed amino-terminal fragment. The single helix anchors it in the bilayer, a juxtamembrane cysteine is a candidate for palmitoylation, and a short basic cytoplasmic tail serves as a binding surface against a channel's cytosolic loops or the acidic inner leaflet. It carries no pore, voltage sensor or catalytic chemistry, so it modulates cation transport from the side rather than conducting itself, shifting the number, gating or kinetics of voltage-gated calcium or potassium channels at the cell surface. The shed fragment may act as a soluble competitor of the same binding sites, tuning channel number after cleavage. Its activity is therefore protein binding at a membrane, its process the regulation of cation transport, and its location the plasma membrane where the channels it modulates sit.

Used as a reviewer, a frontier LLM can compare a dozen generations and distill the most plausible novel hypotheses for scientists to test. To that end, we are open-sourcing an atlas of 33.5 million BioReason-Pro 2 reasoning traces across over 300,000 proteins.

Towards building models we can examine and improve 
Interpretability guided both how we built BioReason-Pro 2 and how we tested its hypotheses. Input interventions exposed coarse use of the ESM3 embeddings, which led us to remove InterPro from training and enrich the granularity of protein grounding in the traces. Internal representations also revealed a scale mismatch between protein and text norms, which we fixed by appropriate rescaling. 
For SMIM32, we tested how protein information influenced a hypothesis at several levels: directly on the modification proposal, through the description of the cysteine, and through earlier reasoning about membrane topology. For RCDG1, SAE features showed which concepts its explanation drew on. Both hypotheses were then validated in the wet lab. 
This kind of analysis matters most for models that combine different kinds of input. Protein embeddings and text annotations can supply overlapping evidence, and each can make different mistakes. Evaluating only the final answer can miss how each input contributes, and where a change in training would help. 
There is further work to do: BioReason-Pro 2 was trained for a particular format for discussing protein function and struggles to follow requests for other formats; interleaving question-answering traces may help broaden that flexibility. Recent results from Tu et al. [19] , showing strong next-amino-acid prediction by Fable 5.2 on little-studied proteins, also motivate evaluating how frontier language models compare on biological hypothesis generation. We plan to include this comparison in our upcoming preprint. 
Our work demonstrates how interpretability can complement end-to-end benchmarks by revealing how models use their biological inputs, identifying limitations that performance metrics alone may miss, and guiding systematic improvements to multimodal biological reasoning models. 
If you are building models for biology or other scientific domains, we’d like to work with you to understand how they use their inputs, develop evaluations that reflect the scientific tasks you care about, and use those findings to guide model improvements.
