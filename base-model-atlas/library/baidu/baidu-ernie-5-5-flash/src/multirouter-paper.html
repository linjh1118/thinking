Expert selection directly affects model capabilities under a fixed active-expert budget. However, dense evaluation of all experts reveals weak agreement between single-router score rankings and rankings from expert-utility evaluations, especially in middle and deep layers. Obtaining richer signals for selection and computation entails additional cost: selection based on internal expert activations requires preliminary computation, while processing multiple full representations separately through the same expert repeats its computation. We propose Multi-View Latent MoE to improve expert selection with multiple routers and further reuse their preferences to modulate expert computation under a fixed expert budget. Its Multi-Router submodule aggregates sigmoid scores from independently initialized routers. These routers develop highly differentiated expert preferences during training, allowing experts strongly favored by one router or jointly supported by several routers to be selected. Multi-View further uses each selected expert’s relative scores across routers to mix learned latent views into its input, reusing routing preferences for both selection and computation. Each selected expert executes one forward pass, preserving the active-expert count, expert FLOPs, and all-to-all communication volume. On a 24-layer 10B-A1B model trained on 708B tokens, full Multi-View Latent MoE improves the mean score across nine downstream tasks by 1.18 percentage points over the baseline. Its Multi-Router submodule alone yields a 0.6-percentage-point gain and improves the rank correlation between router scores and evaluated expert utility, with relative increases of 8.1% globally and approximately 58% in the layer with the lowest single-router alignment. Controlled mixing interventions further show that the model uses input differences that depend on both the expert and the token. Sparse mixture-of-experts (MoE) models expand parameter capacity by activating a small subset of experts for each token ( Shazeer et al., 2017 ; Fedus et al., 2021 ) . This conditional computation enables larger language models under a limited active-computation budget. Routing scores determine which experts process each token and how their outputs are combined. The resulting capability depends on both expert selection and the computation performed by the selected experts. 
Traditional MoE uses a single routing head followed by hard top- k k selection. Dense evaluation of all experts reveals limited agreement between the router’s ranking and the ranking obtained from empirical expert-utility evaluation, especially in middle and deep layers (Fig. 2 ). Unselected experts receive no direct task-loss gradient from that token, so poor selections may become self-reinforcing. This motivates assessing experts from multiple scoring perspectives rather than relying on one router. 
Standard MoE uses routing scores to select experts and weight their outputs, but does not reuse them to construct expert inputs. Load-balancing methods regulate expert utilization ( Wang et al., 2024a ) , but do not themselves provide multiple assessments of each expert. Activation-based routing such as AoE obtains expert-specific signals through partial evaluations of all experts, using low-rank factorizations to control the cost ( Lv et al., 2025 ) . Evaluating multiple input representations separately would also add expert computation. The challenge is therefore to obtain multiple assessments of experts and use routing preferences to modulate their computation while preserving the active-expert count, expert FLOPs, and communication volume. 
We propose Multi-View Latent MoE, a framework that reuses router preferences to improve expert selection and modulate expert computation. Its Multi-Router submodule combines multiple assessments of a shared expert pool: independently initialized sigmoid routing heads produce scores that are summed before one top- k k selection. These routers develop highly differentiated preferences during training. Their aggregation allows experts strongly favored by one router or jointly supported by multiple routers to be selected. This submodule retains a shared expert input while enriching the information used for selection. 
Multi-View further uses these differentiated preferences to modulate each selected expert’s computation. The summed score alone does not retain the routers’ relative preferences: score vectors ( 0.4 , 0.1 ) (0.4,0.1) and ( 0.2 , 0.3 ) (0.2,0.3) both sum to 0.5 0.5 but express opposite preferences (Fig. 1 ). Each router is paired with a learned latent projection, and each selected expert’s raw sigmoid scores are normalized across routers to blend the views into its input before evaluation. The projections are shared across experts, while the mixing proportions depend on both the token and the expert. Mixing takes place before dispatch, and each selected expert performs one forward computation, preserving the active-expert count, expert FLOPs, and all-to-all communication volume. 
On a 24-layer 10B-A1B model trained for 708B tokens, the full Multi-View Latent MoE improves the nine-task downstream mean by 1.18 1.18 percentage points over the baseline, reaching 34.25 34.25 ; its Multi-Router submodule alone provides a 0.6 0.6 -point gain. Routing diagnostics show that this submodule improves global score–utility rank correlation by a relative 8.1 % 8.1\% , with a relative gain of approximately 58 % 58\% in the layer with the lowest single-router alignment (§ 4.4 ). Measurements show distinct expert inputs for the same token. At the final MoE layer, permuting view-mixing proportions across experts while fixing expert selection and output gates raises held-out NLL by 0.01340 0.01340 nats/token; permutation across tokens for the same expert also increases NLL (§ 4.3 ). 2 Related Work Sparse MoE and expert specialization. 
Sparse MoE layers route each token to a small subset of experts and combine their outputs ( Shazeer et al., 2017 ) . Token routing with a linear routing head and top- k k selection remains common in sparse MoE models ( Cai et al., 2024 ; DeepSeek-AI et al., 2024b ; Jiang et al., 2024 ) . Switch Transformer uses top-1 routing ( Fedus et al., 2021 ) . Recent large language models also adopt MoE architectures ( GLM-5-Team et al., 2026 ; Wang et al., 2026a ) . Approaches such as fine-grained experts with an always-active shared expert increase the flexibility of expert specialization ( Dai et al., 2024 ) . In these architectures, however, a discrete routing decision determines the active experts, which process a shared token representation. Router design. 
Router design addresses routing quality, stability, and load balance through methods including router z z -loss ( Zoph et al., 2022 ) , expert-choice routing ( Zhou et al., 2022 ) , auxiliary-loss-free balancing ( Wang et al., 2024a ) , mitigation of representation collapse ( Chi et al., 2022 ) , and attention over experts ( Wu et al., 2024a ) . Expert assignments can fluctuate during training, motivating methods for more stable routing ( Dai et al., 2022 ; Nguyen et al., 2025 ) . Router Upcycling initializes query projections and expert keys from preceding attention layers for dense-to-MoE upcycling, then sums query–key matching scores across routers before softmax selection ( Ran et al., 2025 ) . MoR uses a learnable main router to weight sub-router outputs in LoRA expert mixtures for parameter-efficient fine-tuning ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) . Our Multi-Router submodule sums scores from separately parameterized sigmoid routing heads during pretraining. Multi-View further uses each expert’s relative scores across routing heads to construct its input. Multi-representation and latent-space MoE. 
A separate line of work changes the representations processed by experts. LatentMoE computes router scores from the full hidden state and evaluates experts in a low-dimensional latent space ( Elango et al., 2026 ) . Stable LatentMoE modifies this design for training stability while retaining a single down-projection ( Kimi Team et al., 2026 ) . We build on this shared-projection architecture. MoLAE factorizes expert weights into shared latent mappings, optionally within expert groups, and expert-specific transformations ( Liu et al., 2025b ) . AoE removes the separate router: experts are ranked by the L 2 L^{2} norms of their low-dimensional internal activations, and only selected experts continue computation; low-rank factorization reduces the cost of this initial evaluation ( Lv et al., 2025 ) . MH-MoE splits projected tokens into sub-tokens that share a router and expert pool ( Wu et al., 2024b ; Huang et al., 2024 ) . Multi-Head LatentMoE instead assigns each sub-token an independent MoE module with its own router and experts ( Cui et al., 2026 ) ; SliceMoE also explores token slicing ( Vejendla, 2025 ) . MoH applies shared and routed components to attention heads rather than to the FFN ( Jin et al., 2024 ) . 
Multi-View Latent MoE uses K K projections of the full hidden state as a shared basis. For each selected expert, raw scores are normalized across routers to determine a token- and expert-dependent mixture before expert computation. The number of projections depends on K K , rather than on the number of experts, and expert parameters are shared across views. This changes the representation each expert receives. Prior analyses relate hidden-state geometry to expert-usage patterns ( Wang et al., 2026b ) . Multi-Router retains the shared latent input of LatentMoE while aggregating multiple routing heads; Multi-View further reuses their preferences to modulate expert inputs.

Here b ∈ ℝ N b\in\mathbb{R}^{N} is the backbone’s expert-balancing bias, added only for selection. Output gates normalize the uncorrected score sums over the selected experts and apply the backbone’s routed-output scale: 
g i = α i ​ s ¯ i ∑ j ∈ 𝒮 s ¯ j , i ∈ 𝒮 . g_{i}=\alpha_{i}\frac{\bar{s}_{i}}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}\bar{s}_{j}},\qquad i\in\mathcal{S}. (3) 
Here α i \alpha_{i} is the backbone’s learnable scalar for expert i i ; it scales the expert output, not the view mixture. 
Routing heads are initialized with distinct seeds to break symmetry, without a diversity penalty. We use R = 2 R=2 by default. In Multi-View (§ 3.3 ), each router is paired with one view, so R = K R=K and the two symbols refer to the same count. 
Summation after the sigmoid preserves the routing heads as distinct nonlinear scoring functions. Summing logits before the sigmoid instead gives a single linear scorer, since ∑ r w i ( r ) ⊤ ​ h = ( ∑ r w i ( r ) ) ⊤ ​ h \sum_{r}w_{i}^{(r)\top}h=(\sum_{r}w_{i}^{(r)})^{\top}h . Post-sigmoid summation lets a strong score from one routing head or support across routing heads affect the shared ranking and supplies the relative contributions used for input mixing. Alternatives and their properties appear in Apps. A.3 – A.5 , with training comparisons in § 4.5 .

4 Experiments 
4.1 Setup 
We compare the latent-space MoE baseline, Multi-Router, and score-derived Multi-View on a 24-layer 10B-A1B backbone with top-8 routing and one always-active shared expert ( Elango et al., 2026 ) . The three configurations share the training data, token budget, backbone, and optimization settings (App. B.2 ). Downstream evaluation uses the 708B-token checkpoints on nine tasks (protocol in App. B.4 ). We use R R for the number of routing heads and K K for the number of views. The main method Multi-View( K K ) pairs them one to one, so R = K R=K ; Multi-Router( R R ) uses R R routing heads with a shared expert input. 
Training ablations examine router aggregation, learned input-mixing weights, and view-fed scoring. Their losses are averaged over a ± 1 % \pm 1\% token window around a common ablation checkpoint. A pair of near-matched runs differs after smoothing by at most 1.2 × 10 − 3 1.2\times 10^{-3} , with a mean absolute difference of 7 × 10 − 4 7\times 10^{-4} . 5 Conclusion 
Multi-View Latent MoE reuses routing preferences to improve expert selection and shape expert computation. Motivated by weak agreement between single-router rankings and empirical expert-utility rankings, its Multi-Router submodule combines independently initialized routing heads that learn highly differentiated preferences. Routing diagnostics show relative rank-correlation gains of 8.1 % 8.1\% globally and approximately 58 % 58\% in the layer with the lowest single-router alignment. Multi-View further uses each selected expert’s relative scores across routing heads to blend latent views into its input before dispatch. Each selected expert runs once, preserving the active-expert count, expert FLOPs, and all-to-all communication volume. On a 24-layer 10B-A1B model trained for 708B tokens, full Multi-View Latent MoE improves the nine-task downstream mean by 1.18 1.18 percentage points over the baseline; its Multi-Router submodule alone provides a 0.6 0.6 -point gain. Controlled mixing interventions show that the model makes effective use of expert- and token-dependent input differences. These findings establish routing preferences as a signal for both selecting experts and shaping how they process each token. 
Limitations 
The experiments use a 24-layer 10B-A1B backbone with a shared training seed and evaluate nine downstream tasks at the 708B-token checkpoint. The controlled interventions measure the trained model’s use of view mixing at that checkpoint.

A.1 LatentMoE as the backbone 
Because the experts operate in latent space, replacing a selected expert’s input z z with a view v ( m ) v^{(m)} leaves its architecture, width, and hyperparameters unchanged. The existing expert blocks can therefore be reused without increasing expert capacity. Latent-space designs have also been studied in other MoE architectures ( Elango et al., 2026 ; Liu et al., 2025b ) . 
A.2 Contextual representations and expert inputs 
Attention produces a contextual representation h h , but the baseline MoE still evaluates all k k selected experts at the same projected input z = P ​ h z=Ph . The attention representation and the expert inputs are separate architectural choices: multiple projections can give the selected experts different inputs derived from the same h h . We use the term view for this learned latent projection or the resulting expert input.

For post-sigmoid fusion, different aggregation operators emphasize different score patterns. Let s m ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) s_{m}\in(0,1) denote one router’s score for an expert. An additive operator, ∑ m s m \sum_{m}s_{m} , allows a high score from one router to increase the aggregate even when other scores are low. Multiplicative operators, ∏ m s m \prod_{m}s_{m} and the geometric mean ( ∏ m s m ) 1 / R (\prod_{m}s_{m})^{1/R} , suppress an expert when any router assigns it a near-zero score. We also consider additive operators with an agreement term, ∑ m s m + λ ​ ∏ m s m \sum_{m}s_{m}+\lambda\prod_{m}s_{m} , or a disagreement penalty, ∑ m s m − λ ​ | s 1 − s 2 | \sum_{m}s_{m}-\lambda\,|s_{1}-s_{2}| ; both reduce to summation at λ = 0 \lambda=0 . 
Summation has two useful properties, with empirical comparisons in Table 3 and Fig. 7 . First, multiplicative aggregation is sensitive to low scores: one near-zero score can suppress an expert regardless of the other routers’ scores. Summation does not impose this requirement for agreement. Second, with s ¯ i = ∑ m s m , i \bar{s}_{i}=\sum_{m}s_{m,i} , the expert’s output gate in Eq. 3 can be decomposed into per-router contributions, g i = ∑ m α i ​ s m , i / ∑ j ∈ 𝒮 s ¯ j g_{i}=\sum_{m}\alpha_{i}s_{m,i}/\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}\bar{s}_{j} . A product does not have this additive decomposition, and its scale depends on R R . The same raw sigmoid scores also supply the input-mixing weights in § 3.3 : for each selected expert, its scores are normalized across routers, a m , i = s m , i / ∑ n s n , i a_{m,i}=s_{m,i}/\sum_{n}s_{n,i} , so that ∑ m a m , i = 1 \sum_{m}a_{m,i}=1 . 
We also evaluate a variant that selects experts using max m ⁡ s m , i \max_{m}s_{m,i} and gates their outputs using ∑ m s m , i \sum_{m}s_{m,i} . Selection therefore depends on an expert’s highest router score, while the output weight uses scores from all routers. This separates the effects of score aggregation on selection and gating. Conceptually, it corresponds to choosing one router at the selection stage, analogous to choosing one view for an expert’s input in a hard single-view variant. A.5 Aggregation before selection 
The layer aggregates all router scores before a single top- k k selection. Every router scores the full expert set { 1 , … , N } \{1,\dots,N\} , and the number of activated experts remains k k , independent of the number R R of routers or K K of views. All routing heads contribute to one shared ranking over the full expert pool. 
An alternative is to distribute a fixed budget of k k selections adaptively among routers. Although this preserves the total budget, each router still ranks experts independently and selects only up to its assigned quota. An expert may therefore be excluded from each local selection even if its aggregate score would place it in the global top- k k . We instead aggregate the scores and perform one selection with a fixed total of k k experts.

A.7 Implementation 
Standard MoE implementations permute tokens before all-to-all communication, copying each token’s latent vector once per selected expert into a buffer organized by expert. Multi-View instead constructs the d d -dimensional mixed input v ^ i = ∑ m a m , i ​ v ( m ) \hat{v}_{i}=\sum_{m}a_{m,i}v^{(m)} for each selected (token, expert) pair on the token’s device before dispatch. For T T tokens, the implementation flattens the T × k T\times k token–expert slots, each with one selected expert as its destination. Each token still dispatches k k vectors of dimension d d , so the all-to-all volume is unchanged. Dispatching all K K views and combining them on the expert’s device would instead multiply that volume by K K . The return all-to-all communication, inverse permutation, and gated sum of expert outputs are unchanged. 
Because selection uses no per-view quota, load balancing is applied once over the N N experts using the backbone’s existing scheme. No auxiliary loss is added for the views. 
The expert bias is added after summing the routing-head scores and is used only by top- k k selection, 𝒮 = TopK k ​ ( s ¯ + b ) \mathcal{S}=\mathrm{TopK}_{k}(\bar{s}+b) ; it enters neither the input-mixing weights nor the output gates. The output gates follow Eq. 3 : the uncorrected s ¯ i \bar{s}_{i} are normalized over the selected set and then multiplied by the backbone’s per-expert output scale. For input mixing, Eq. 4 normalizes each selected expert’s raw sigmoid scores across routers, and the resulting coefficients directly form v ^ i = ∑ m a m , i ​ v ( m ) \hat{v}_{i}=\sum_{m}a_{m,i}v^{(m)} . The input-ratio computation uses ϵ = 10 − 9 \epsilon=10^{-9} for numerical stability, omitted from the mathematical formulas. At fixed parameters, replacing the score sum by its mean and scaling b b by 1 / R 1/R preserves selection, output gates, and view weights. To maintain the bias correspondence during training, its initialization and update increments must also be scaled by 1 / R 1/R . In Multi-Router, repeating one routing head R R times and multiplying its balancing bias by R R preserves the forward computation with a single routing head. Execution-path verification.

A.8 Interaction with other components 
Multi-View modifies the MoE down-projection, router, and permutation step. Attention, including multi-head latent attention (MLA) ( DeepSeek-AI et al., 2024a ) , is unchanged, as is the KV cache. Hyper-connections and their manifold-constrained variant mHC use multiple residual streams with learned mixing between sublayers ( Zhu et al., 2024 ; Xie et al., 2025 ) . Multi-View accepts the resulting sublayer input h h and returns a D D -dimensional output, so these designs are architecturally compatible. Multi-token prediction (MTP) ( DeepSeek-AI et al., 2024b ) adds prediction modules and a loss term, while Multi-View leaves the training objective unchanged. In our configuration, the MTP module uses a dense FFN and is not modified. The method is also compatible in design with fine-grained and shared experts, auxiliary-loss-free balancing, and dense or hash-routed early layers ( Roller et al., 2021 ) . It leaves the vocabulary, embeddings, routed and active expert counts, expert blocks, up-projection, and expert-parallel layout unchanged. 
A.9 Warm-starting from a LatentMoE checkpoint 
An existing LatentMoE checkpoint provides a possible initialization for Multi-View. View 1 retains the checkpoint’s down-projection and routing head. The remaining views are initialized from the same projection with small perturbations, and their routing heads are initialized to zero. These additional routing heads add the same constant to every expert’s raw score, preserving the raw-score ordering but generally changing normalized output gates. Keeping the balancing bias b b unchanged also preserves the actual bias-corrected selection, because the same constant is added to every entry of s ¯ + b \bar{s}+b . The views can then differentiate during continued training.
