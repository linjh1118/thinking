Large language models (LLMs) ( Achiam et al., 2023 ; Touvron et al., 2023 ; Bai et al., 2023 ; Liu et al., 2024a ) are predominantly trained with the Next-Token Prediction (NTP) objective.
While NTP has demonstrated remarkable scalability and effectiveness,
it provides supervision only at the immediate next-token horizon,
leaving potentially useful future information unexploited.
Moreover, autoregressive decoding remains inherently sequential,
resulting in limited inference throughput. 
To better utilize future information,
recent work has explored Multi-Token Prediction (MTP)
as an auxiliary objective on top of NTP.
By introducing additional future-token prediction targets,
MTP provides denser supervision during training and can generate draft tokens for speculative decoding, enabling inference acceleration. 
Early MTP methods predict multiple future positions independently from the same hidden state Gloeckle et al. (2024) , providing denser supervision but ignoring causal dependencies among future tokens. Subsequent causal MTP methods such as DeepSeek-V3 Liu et al. (2024a) address this limitation by introducing autoregressive dependencies among future prediction steps, significantly improving prediction quality and improving the backbone model’s overall performance Liu et al. (2024a) ; Blakeman et al. (2025) ; Zeng et al. (2026) . 
However, both causal and parallel MTP remain auxiliary by construction: future-token prediction is carried out by dedicated modules attached to the backbone, rather than being integrated into the autoregressive generation process itself. This leads to two limitations: 
1. 
Indirect Multi-Horizon Supervision 
MTP losses primarily supervise the auxiliary prediction modules.
Consequently, the backbone model receives multi-horizon learning signals only indirectly, potentially limiting their impact on long-horizon planning. 
2. 
Costly Long-Horizon Supervision 
Denser supervision requires deeper prediction horizons, yet each additional horizon adds a prediction module in memory and another pass in compute.
This cost limits the prediction horizon in large-scale causal MTP pretraining, typically to a single draft token. 
To address the above limitations, we introduce Efficient Causal Self-Recursion (ECHO) , a causal self-recursive
framework that reuses the backbone’s final layer for future prediction and
samples per-step prediction depths with decaying probabilities to reduce the amortized MTP training overhead. 
Final-layer parameter sharing ensures that multi-horizon losses directly shape the backbone, rather than being absorbed by horizon-specific auxiliary modules.
As shown in Figure 3(a) , this allows ECHO to benefit from deeper supervision: its downstream performance continues to improve at depth 3, whereas DeepSeek’s MTP ( Liu et al., 2024a ) does not, consistent with observations in MiMo ( LLM-Core Xiaomi, 2025 ) .
More fundamentally, training the same parameters responsible for next-token generation to predict multiple future tokens encourages the backbone to anticipate how the sequence unfolds over subsequent steps, as evidenced by ECHO’s substantial gains on reasoning tasks. 
To make long-horizon supervision computationally efficient, we introduce Dynamic Depth Sampling (DDS) , which samples prediction depths with decaying probabilities. This simultaneously reduces the amortized MTP training overhead and mitigates the impact of increasing prediction errors and supervision noise at greater depths. After supervised finetuning, ECHO’s advantage grows further. 
Beyond its pretraining benefits, ECHO entirely eliminates the need for post-hoc drafter finetuning . DDS makes longer-horizon supervision affordable, while final-layer parameter sharing enables depth extrapolation: since the same shared layer is reused at every draft step, ECHO can extend drafting beyond the trained prediction depth while maintaining high acceptance rates, at an amortized MTP training overhead of only 1.5 depth-1 steps (see Section 4.1 ). By drafting directly with its pretrained weights, ECHO avoids the overfitting to academic benchmarks associated with post-hoc drafter finetuning ( Liu et al., 2026 ) , yielding substantial acceptance-length gains in realistic, high-entropy generation settings as shown in Figure 3(b) . 
Our main contributions are summarized as follows: 
• 
We introduce Efficient Causal Self-Recursion (ECHO) , which predicts every future horizon by recursively applying the backbone’s own final layer, with the per-step prediction depth drawn from a distribution decaying along depth. 
• 
ECHO imposes direct multi-horizon supervision, beating auxiliary MTP on reasoning and code at matched depth while holding on QA & multiple choice, and widening its lead on both classes after supervised finetuning. 
• 
ECHO makes long-horizon prediction cheap in compute and free in parameters, and keeps gaining acceptance when extrapolating in depth, where auxiliary MTP saturates. It eliminates drafter finetuning entirely, yet outperforms post-hoc drafters, especially on open-ended generation. 
2 Related Work 
2.1 Multi-Token Prediction 
Multi-Token Prediction (MTP) augments next-token prediction with future-token targets, improving language modeling while providing draft tokens for speculative decoding. It inherits the architecture of speculative draft models and differs in the training regime: draft models are finetuned post-hoc against a frozen backbone on a limited dataset, whereas MTP modules are optimized jointly with the backbone throughout pretraining. 
Parallel MTP predicts multiple future positions independently from several output heads on a shared backbone Gloeckle et al. (2024) , following Medusa Cai et al. (2024) . Causal MTP instead follows EAGLE Li et al. (2024) : DeepSeek-V3 Liu et al. (2024a) feeds dedicated prediction modules the previous hidden state together with shifted token embeddings, preserving autoregressive dependencies among future steps, and GLM-5 Zeng et al. (2026) shares those parameters across all depths, as EAGLE does, to reduce memory cost. 
2.2 Post-hoc Drafter 
Post-hoc drafters are trained on top of a frozen target model by finetuning on target-generated responses, and differ mainly in how a block of draft tokens is produced.
Medusa Cai et al. (2024) attaches parallel heads that draft every position from the final hidden state, while EAGLE-3 Li et al. (2025) re-enters a lightweight decoder once per drafted token, conditioned on target hidden states, preserving token-to-token dependency.
Recent parallel drafters build on DFlash Chen et al. (2026) , which predicts the whole block in one pass, with DSpark Cheng et al. (2026) restoring intra-block dependency through a lightweight causal head. 
Two limitations are nonetheless inherent to post-hoc drafting.
First, the drafter is narrowly aligned with the target, being finetuned on a dataset orders of magnitude smaller than the pretraining corpus.
Second, scaling that training to the pretraining corpus is compute-heavy from the multi-step or multi-layer overhead, which has not been shown to improve the backbone’s downstream performance and may even degrade training.
ECHO overcomes both: joint pretraining aligns it with the target, and its supervision improves the backbone at modest overhead. 
2.3 Looped Transformers 
Looped transformers reuse a shared block of layers across iterations, buying computational depth without new parameters: parameter efficiency ( Lan et al., 2020 ) , adaptive computation ( Dehghani et al., 2019 ) , reasoning at LLM scale ( Saunshi et al., 2025 ; Zhu et al., 2025 ) , test-time compute scaling ( Geiping et al., 2025 ) , and depth allocated per token ( Bae et al., 2025 ) . In all of them recursion refines the same token position under a next-token objective; ECHO instead recurses along the horizon, each step conditioning on the next future token to predict one position further ahead. 
Yet both buy structure rather than recall, and for looping that is a trade: at matched parameters and compute it leads on reasoning but trails on perplexity and closed-book QA ( Saunshi et al., 2025 ) , and at scale its gain is credited to knowledge manipulation rather than capacity, measured against external dense families rather than a same-recipe baseline ( Zhu et al., 2025 ) . ECHO pays no such price: mathematics, reasoning and code gain while multiple choice and closed-book QA hold ( Figure 3(a) ), and both classes gain after finetuning ( Figure 4(b) ). Looping, latent chain-of-thought ( Hao et al., 2025 ) and ECHO thus price latent computation differently: repeated passes over one position, annotated chains, and extra positions — the one price that accepted tokens can repay. 4 Pushing the Frontier of Speculative Decoding under Realistic Serving 
We analyze how ECHO’s end-to-end paradigm intrinsically aligns the drafter with the target, entirely eliminating the need for post-hoc drafter finetuning.

As shown in Figure 3(b) , ECHO and other causal drafters outperform parallel drafters on high-entropy tasks, consistent with Liu et al. (2026) ; Figure 8 shows the same ordering in decode speedup.
When the next tokens are uncertain, the target’s choice is hard to anticipate, so the probability of acceptance falls off steeply with depth and most of a long draft is discarded.
A causal drafter adapts to this by extending its draft step by step, while a parallel drafter emits a fixed-length block whose deeper tokens are mostly rejected at verification.
The balance shifts on simple code and mathematics benchmarks such as HumanEval, MBPP, and GSM8K, where long stretches of output are determined by syntax or derivation, and block-parallel drafting becomes worthwhile ( Liu et al., 2026 ) , whereas realistic software engineering tasks such as SWE-bench remain high-entropy and still favor causal drafting ( Figure 3(b) ).
Such simple tasks, however, are a small fraction of real-world usage, account for under 8% of consumer ChatGPT messages, while open-ended practical guidance, information seeking, and writing account for nearly 80% ( Chatterji et al., 2025 ) . Joint Pretraining Closes the Alignment Gap. 
Acceptance depends on how closely the draft distribution matches the target’s ( Leviathan et al., 2023 ; Zhou et al., 2024 ) , and a drafter can better match the target where it has seen the target’s data. ECHO is trained with the target end-to-end and sees its full pretraining corpus. In contrast, auxiliary drafters are fit to a frozen target on a small finetuning set, so their acceptance drops under distribution shift ( Liu et al., 2024b ; Hong et al., 2025 ) .
The EAGLE-3 pair in Figure 3(b) isolates this effect: with architecture and finetuning data held fixed, warm-started EAGLE-3 achieves a longer accepted length than its cold-start counterpart, consistent with recent findings that pretrained initialization strengthens drafters, most on out-of-domain inputs ( Bie et al., 2026 ) .
Yet on open-ended tasks, warm-starting still falls short of ECHO, with the largest gap on SWE-bench.
ECHO is instead optimized jointly with the target on every pretraining update, so its alignment is maintained throughout training, in line with the high acceptance rates reported for MTP modules trained jointly during pretraining ( Liu et al., 2024a ; LLM-Core Xiaomi, 2025 ) .

Supervised Finetuning : Every configuration is finetuned with the same recipe, 5,000 steps over one pass of an in-house chat corpus at context length 32,768, keeping its pretrained MTP depths. 
Computational Cost :
Each pretraining run takes approximately 3 days on 192 NVIDIA H800 GPUs with a global batch size of 1,920, followed by 5 hours of SFT on the same hardware. Post-hoc drafter training uses 24 H800 GPUs with a global batch size of 48, requiring about 22 hours for EAGLE-3, 19 hours for DFlash, and 55 hours for DSpark. 
Benchmark Evaluation : Fourteen few-shot ( Brown et al., 2020 ) benchmarks decoded greedily, grouped by task form into mathematics, reasoning, multi-subject multiple choice, code and closed-book question answering, with CoT prompting ( Wei et al., 2022 ) on MATH, GSM8K and BBH. 
Speculative decoding: We adopt the same temperature setting ( T = 1 T=1 ) for both the target and draft models, following Bebop ( Li et al., 2026b ) and GLM-5.2 ( GLM Team, 2026 ) . This matters for RL training: rejection sampling with draft temperature > 0 >0 keeps acceptance stable as target entropy rises, whereas greedy drafting degrades sharply at higher temperatures ( Li et al., 2026b ) . All drafters are served with SGLang on a single node of eight H800 GPUs and budget-matched at seven proposed positions plus one bonus token per verification step. We report decode-only throughput and mean accepted length on ten tasks, split by the target model’s predictive entropy into predictable and open-ended groups. 
7 Conclusion 
We presented ECHO, which turns multi-token prediction from an auxiliary task into part of the autoregressive objective.
By reusing the backbone’s own final layer at every horizon, ECHO lets multi-horizon supervision shape the parameters that generate the next token, and Dynamic Depth Sampling makes deeper horizons affordable by training them less often.
Unlike auxiliary MTP such as DeepSeek, ECHO improves with added depth, and ECHO-1.5 (3) matches the downstream gain of ECHO-3 at half the MTP overhead. 
At inference, the same shared layer makes the pretrained model its own drafter with close draft-target alignment: it drafts beyond its trained depth where auxiliary modules saturate, and it outperforms post-hoc drafters on open-ended, high-entropy generation.
This advantage holds under a doubled token budget, a repeated pretraining run, and supervised finetuning. 
Limitations 
Due to compute constraints, pretraining was repeated once and finetuning was run with a single seed.
How far the gains from added depth and from depth extrapolation extend, how DDS behaves at greater depths, and why deeper sampling pays off only after finetuning are left to future work.

Appendix A Experiment Setup 
A.1 Pretraining 
Model Architecture : The backbone is a 10B-A1B Latent Mixture-of-Experts transformer ( Blakeman et al., 2025 ) , that is 10B parameters in total with 1B activated per token, stacked as 24 layers of hidden size 1,024 with 16 attention heads. Attention is multi-head latent ( Liu et al., 2024a ) , with 512-dimensional query and key–value latents, and we add gated attention ( Qiu et al., 2025 ) , per-head query–key normalization and rotary positional embeddings ( Su et al., 2024 ) . Every layer is a mixture of experts in a latent space: the expert block projects the hidden state down to 512 dimensions, runs 256 fine-grained routed experts ( Dai et al., 2024 ) of intermediate size 1,024 there, and projects back, with the router reading the hidden state rather than the latent. A sigmoid gate sends each token to eight routed experts, balanced by a sequence-level auxiliary loss, plus one shared expert. The SwiGLU ( Shazeer, 2020 ) feed-forward width is 5,120 and the vocabulary holds 201,216 tokens. 
Training Settings : We train the model on 600B tokens with a context sequence length of 4,096 and a global batch size of 1,920. Training uses bfloat16 precision and the Muon optimizer ( Jordan et al., 2024 ) , with AdamW retained for the embeddings, the biases, the output head and the expert router, a learning rate of 6 × 10 − 4 6\times 10^{-4} , a warmup of 1,000 steps, weight decay of 0.1 0.1 , and gradient clipping at 1.0 1.0 . Following Liu et al. (2024a) , we set the MTP loss scaling factor to 0.3 0.3 . Pretraining runs on 24 nodes of eight H800 GPUs each, with eight-way expert parallelism, stage-one optimizer sharding, no tensor or pipeline parallelism, and full activation recomputation. 
Computational Cost :
Each pretraining run is conducted on 24 nodes with eight NVIDIA H800 80GB GPUs per node (192 GPUs in total), taking approximately three days to complete. Supervised finetuning uses the same hardware configuration and takes approximately five hours per run.
For the post-hoc speculative decoding baselines, we train the draft models on three nodes (24 H800 80GB GPUs), using a global batch size of 48. EAGLE-3 (cold-start and warm-start) and DFlash use a per-GPU batch size of 2 without gradient accumulation, whereas DSpark uses a per-GPU batch size of 1 with two gradient accumulation steps due to GPU memory constraints. EAGLE-3 requires approximately 22 hours per run in measured wall-clock time. Based on the recorded compute time and an estimated 1.27 × \times overhead for startup, checkpointing, and cluster variability, DSpark requires approximately 55 hours, while DFlash requires approximately 19 hours. 
Methods Compared : All six configurations are trained under the recipe above, so the comparison isolates the MTP design. ECHO reuses the backbone’s last layer as the body of every MTP depth; DeepSeek’s MTP baselines follow Liu et al. (2024a) and keep an independent module per depth, sharing only the embedding and the output head. ECHO-1 and MTP-1 train one depth, ECHO-3 and MTP-3 train three at every step, ECHO-1.5 (3) samples depths one to three with 𝔼 ⁡ [ D ] = 1.5 \mathbb{E}[D]=1.5 , and ECHO-2 (7) extends dynamic depth sampling to depths one through seven with 𝔼 ⁡ [ D ] = 2 \mathbb{E}[D]=2 . The two cost-matched comparisons are ECHO-1 against MTP-1 at equal depth-one cost, and ECHO-1.5 (3) against MTP-3 at half the MTP cost. 
Dynamic Depth Sampling : The sampled depth is D ∼ π D\sim\pi , where π \pi places probabilities 0.6, 0.3 and 0.1 on depths 1, 2 and 3, so 𝔼 ⁡ [ D ] = 1.5 \mathbb{E}[D]=1.5 . Each optimizer step draws one D D and trains depths 1 through D D , and every micro-batch in the step shares the same D D , which keeps per-step compute constant. Averaging the loss over the D D trained depths gives depth d d the weight 𝔼 [ 𝟏 { D ≥ d } / D ] \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{D\geq d\}/D] , and these weights sum to one: ECHO reweights depths by how often it samples them, not by attaching a per-depth loss coefficient. D D is a deterministic function of the seed and the step index, so every rank derives it without communication and a resumed run continues the same sequence. 
Supervised Finetuning : All configurations adopt an identical recipe: 5,000 steps over one pass of an in-house chat SFT corpus at a context length of 32,768, with eight-way context parallelism. Each keeps the MTP depths it was pretrained with, so the finetuned comparison isolates the same design difference as the pretrained one. 
A.2 Benchmark Evaluation 
Evaluation Setup : We evaluate the pretrained checkpoints on fourteen few-shot benchmarks, grouped by the form of the task following Liu et al. (2024a) : mathematics with MATH ( Hendrycks et al., 2021b ) and GSM8K ( Cobbe et al., 2021 ) ; reasoning with BBH ( Suzgun et al., 2023 ) , BBEH ( Kazemi et al., 2025 ) , WinoGrande ( Sakaguchi et al., 2021 ) and PIQA ( Bisk et al., 2020 ) ; multi-subject multiple choice with MMLU ( Hendrycks et al., 2021a ) and MMLU-Pro ( Wang et al., 2024 ) ; code with HumanEval+ and MBPP+ ( Liu et al., 2023 ) and LiveCodeBench ( Jain et al., 2025 ) ; closed-book question answering with PopQA ( Mallen et al., 2023 ) , PreciseWikiQA ( Bang et al., 2025 ) and HotpotQA ( Yang et al., 2018 ) . We report unweighted averages within each group and across all fourteen benchmarks. Benchmarks such as GPQA ( Rein et al., 2024 ) , SimpleQA ( Wei et al., 2024 ) and HMMT are left out for lack of resolution rather than for any defect in their scoring: every configuration scores near their floor, so the row moves more than it resolves. 
Prompts and Decoding : All benchmarks are evaluated in the few-shot in-context format ( Brown et al., 2020 ) . Chain-of-thought prompting ( Wei et al., 2022 ) is used on MATH, GSM8K and BBH, with eight, eight and four demonstrations respectively. The remaining benchmarks are prompted to answer directly, with five demonstrations for the multiple-choice benchmarks, ten for closed-book question answering and two for BBEH. BBH is released under both a chain-of-thought and an answer-directly prompt over the same items for examining the chain-of-thought effect ( Suzgun et al., 2023 ) ; we report its chain-of-thought form in the main results. Decoding is greedy throughout. Generation is capped per benchmark: two tokens for MMLU and eleven for MMLU-Pro, which are answered with a single option label; 65 65 for the closed-book sets and 101 101 for PopQA; 401 401 for MATH, 512 512 for GSM8K, BBEH and HumanEval+, 601 601 for BBH, 768 768 for LiveCodeBench and 8192 8192 for MBPP+. WinoGrande and PIQA involve no generation. Stop sequences are applied when the answer is extracted rather than during decoding, so the cap is what bounds each output. 
Scoring and Averaging : Each benchmark’s score is an unweighted macro-average over its sub-tasks rather than a per-item micro-average. Four scoring rules are used, one per group of benchmarks. GSM8K, MATH, BBH, MMLU and MMLU-Pro are scored by rule from the extracted answer. HumanEval+, MBPP+ and LiveCodeBench are scored as pass@1 by execution ( Chen et al., 2021 ) . WinoGrande and PIQA are scored by length-normalized log-likelihood over the candidate continuations rather than by generation, as in Liu et al. (2024a) , with the normalization of Gao et al. (2024) . PreciseWikiQA, HotpotQA, PopQA and BBEH are graded against the reference answer by an LLM judge. In Table 1 the three factual-knowledge sets are graded by gpt-oss-120b and BBEH by Claude Opus 4.8; in Table 3 all four are graded by Claude Opus 4.8. Of these, the three factual-knowledge sets report the F1 of overall accuracy and accuracy among attempted items, with abstentions in the denominator of the former only. Category averages and the overall figure are unweighted means over benchmarks. 
A.3 Speculative Decoding under Serving 
Serving Stack : All serving measurements use SGLang on one node of eight H800 GPUs, with tensor parallelism 8 and FlashAttention-3. 
Drafters : All drafters are budget-matched at seven proposed positions plus one target bonus token, i.e. eight verification slots per request per step. ECHO reuses the target’s final layer and full output head. EAGLE-3 ( Li et al., 2025 ) uses one draft layer with a reduced 32,000-entry head scattered back to target width, in a cold-start and a warm-start variant; following Kimi Team (2026) , the warm start initializes the draft from DeepSeek’s pretrained MTP weights, and is always normalized by the baseline of its own run. DSpark ( Cheng et al., 2026 ) and DFlash ( Chen et al., 2026 ) are block-parallel with five draft layers, the former adding a low-rank Markov transition bias. 
Serving Metric : We report decode-only throughput, decoded tokens over summed per-request decode latency, which excludes prefill; metrics that include prefill confound the comparison across tasks with differing prompt lengths. Speedup is recomputed against the baseline of the same run, and accepted length is decoded tokens per verification step. 
Tasks : Serving results use ten tasks in two groups, split by predictive entropy as in Liu et al. (2026) . Predictable, following the commonly adopted SpecForge benchmarks ( Li et al., 2026a ) : HumanEval, MBPP, MATH-500, GSM8K, LiveCodeBench. Open-ended: SWE-bench, Arena-Hard, MT-Bench, summarization, Alpaca. Question answering, retrieval-augmented generation, translation and AIME-25 are excluded, all having median outputs of twenty to thirty tokens, too few verification steps for a stable decode-only ratio. The entropy axis is the target’s own predictive entropy, measured separately. 
Verification Budget : DSpark is evaluated with its confidence scheduler disabled, verifying the full draft block at every step, as in the diagnostic baseline of Cheng et al. (2026) . The scheduler is a calibrated cost model rather than a threshold: it needs a latency curve re-profiled for each GPU and backend ( Cheng et al., 2026 ; Liu et al., 2026 ) , and a confidence head calibrated on the serving distribution, since the survival probabilities it multiplies must be absolute rather than merely well-ranked. That calibration does not transfer, because the mechanism assumes acceptance varies sharply across domains in the first place. With the head enabled but neither artifact available, per-step cost rose 5–14% and nothing was pruned. 
Depth Extrapolation : The depth sweep of Figure 1(b) uses a separate harness.
