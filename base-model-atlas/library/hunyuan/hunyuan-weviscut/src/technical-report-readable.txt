Producing long-form videos from narration requires interdependent decisions
that remain coherent across shots. Existing agentic editors often use
multi-stage workflows driven by general models, leaving the quality-cost
trade-off of compact, task-adapted policies unclear.
We address this gap with WeVisCut , a long-form script-to-video system
that connects tool execution with policy learning. Its agent harness maintains
shared production state so that tool feedback informs subsequent decisions
and repairs.
Because finished videos do not expose these decisions, our four-stage data
pipeline combines editing inversion with verified replay to turn recovered
editing intent into executable supervision. Applied to 3,750 finished videos,
this pipeline yields over 130K training examples.
We use this supervision to train a single 9B policy that coordinates
production and recovers from failures within the same harness.
Moreover, we introduce a 100-task benchmark and five metrics assessing editing
quality and delivery reliability.
Under a shared tool environment, WeVisCut substantially outperforms its base
model and achieves comparable performance to Claude Opus 5 while using nearly 33 $\times$ fewer generated tokens per successful output minute.
It also outperforms task-matched native editors on our proposed metrics.

1 Introduction

“The whole eloquence of cinema is achieved in the editing
room.”

— Orson Welles

Creating a narrated long-form video involves turning a script into a
sequence of coherent shots. Planning, visual retrieval, narration synthesis,
and timeline assembly are interdependent. Changing a shot’s duration affects
later timestamps, while unsuitable visual material may require both retrieval
and planning to be revisited across the affected shot sequence.

Recent agentic editors coordinate production through specialized agents and
tool workflows [ 48 , 39 , 47 , 49 , 21 , 44 ] . Several systems use general
models with feedback or graph refinement. Yet orchestration alone does not
specify how to train a compact policy that maintains video quality at low
token cost. Crayotter trains a 9B editing model using final-video preferences
on manually constructed tasks [ 38 ] .
Such a policy needs a stateful environment for execution and recovery, together with
supervision grounded in that environment. Finished videos, however, omit the
tool calls, intermediate artifacts, and failures needed for such supervision.
The challenge is therefore
to connect stateful execution, executable supervision, and policy learning.

To address these challenges, we present WeVisCut, which integrates a
script-to-video agent harness, executable data construction, and a task-adapted
policy. The harness (Figure 2 ) maintains shared production
state and validates tool actions. It exposes execution feedback for inspection,
bounded repair, and replanning while preserving unaffected artifacts
from earlier successful production steps.

Within this harness, we construct executable supervision through the
four-stage pipeline in Figure 3 . We first recover script–shot
intent through editing inversion and execute it through verified replay.
When replay reveals defects, we apply observe–reflect–repair for bounded
corrections that preserve successful artifacts. We then use trajectory
materialization to organize the recorded executions into training examples
with tool feedback and repair outcomes. From 3,750 video projects, this
process yields over 130K examples.

Using these examples, we train a single 9B policy across production roles in
three phases (Figure 4 ). In Phase 1, we establish tool use
and recovery through unified supervised fine-tuning [ 46 ] and runtime-verified rejection-sampled fine-tuning [ 45 ] .
In Phase 2, we apply Guided-OPD [ 12 ] to learn recovery on
policy-reached states while phasing out teacher intervention.
In Phase 3, we use RCF-DAPO , our role- and context-factored
DAPO [ 43 ] , to optimize decisions by comparing runtime outcomes
within matched roles and contexts.

Moreover, we introduce a benchmark of 100 script-to-video tasks and five complementary
metrics to evaluate editing quality and delivery reliability. These metrics
relate rendered videos to their scripts and production plans, so evaluation
goes beyond successful rendering alone. In a shared tool environment, our
policy improves over its base model by 16.0 points in Overall Score
and achieves comparable performance to Claude Opus 5 while using nearly 33 $\times$ fewer generated tokens per successful output minute.
Our complete system also outperforms task-matched native editors.

We summarize our contributions as follows.

We design a script-to-video agent harness that maintains shared
production state, validates tool actions, and supports inspection,
bounded repair, and final delivery.

We build a four-stage data pipeline based on editing inversion and
verified replay, converting finished videos into executable training examples.

We train a unified 9B policy for tool use and recovery through
sequential SFT, runtime-verified RFT, Guided-OPD, and RCF-DAPO within a shared production harness.

We introduce a 100-task benchmark and five metrics. WeVisCut improves
its base policy, outperforms task-matched systems, and achieves comparable
quality to Claude Opus 5 at markedly lower cost.

2 Related Work

Tool agents and long-horizon post-training. Producing long-form videos requires agents to choose tools and revise plans
from execution feedback. Earlier methods range from interleaved reasoning and
action [ 41 ] to learned API calls [ 29 , 23 , 22 ] and runnable
tool dialogues [ 13 ] . To
train such behavior, researchers curate interactions and synthesize
instructions or tool calls [ 46 , 2 , 20 , 16 ] . Reflection and reusable skills also
support recovery [ 32 , 35 ] . Closer to our
data pipeline, AgentTrek builds verified web trajectories from
tutorials [ 37 ] . Finished videos, however, do not record
editing actions. We therefore infer script–shot intent and replay it with
production tools to obtain executable trajectories. Long-horizon training
also needs to handle policy-generated states. DAgger [ 28 ] collects expert labels for
learner states, while generalized
knowledge distillation trains on student-generated sequences [ 1 ] . Guided-OPD mixes teacher and student
turns, while TurnOPD and Step-Level OPD refine supervision at turn and step
boundaries [ 12 , 51 , 33 ] . We apply
Guided-OPD with truncated teacher logits. For reward optimization, GRPO and
DAPO build on PPO-style updates with group-relative comparisons [ 30 , 31 , 43 ] . We adapt DAPO for
our multi-role policy as RCF-DAPO , comparing
rewards only within matched roles and decision contexts.

Video editing. Video editing systems range from user-assisted tools to autonomous agents.
LAVE keeps users in the editing loop [ 34 ] . In more automated
settings, CutClaw and CineAgents handle music-synchronized editing and
cinematic compilation [ 48 , 47 ] .
The original Crayotter uses a traceable multi-agent workflow, while VideoAgent
assembles specialists dynamically [ 39 , 49 ] . Beyond
workflow design, task-specific training has also been explored. “The Script
is All You Need” trains a ScripterAgent but uses a separate DirectorAgent [ 21 ] . Aurora trains a planner for local edits [ 44 ] . Building on the Crayotter environment, subsequent work
trains a 9B policy with final-video preferences on manually constructed
tasks [ 38 ] . In contrast, WeVisCut learns one role-routed
policy for fixed-narration production from executable trajectories built
through editing inversion, verified replay, observe–reflect–repair, and
trajectory materialization.

Agent and video evaluation. Evaluation of narration-driven video production needs to consider both the
rendered video and the production process. VBench, VideoScore, and VMBench
assess generated-video quality and motion [ 9 , 6 , 15 ] , while Goku-Bench
tests instruction-based edits [ 14 ] . Script–video timing has
also been evaluated [ 21 ] . Complementary agent evaluation
ranges from recorded web and mobile trajectories [ 3 , 27 ] to interactive shopping, browser,
and desktop environments that validate action outcomes [ 40 , 50 , 11 , 4 , 36 ] . Software-repair benchmarks use repository
tests [ 10 ] , while multi-turn and tool-oriented suites
measure progress, valid transitions, and task constraints [ 17 , 19 , 18 , 42 ] .
We combine these perspectives in a benchmark of 100 script-to-video tasks,
linking scripts, shot plans, tool trajectories, and rendered videos. Five
metrics are introduced to assess script fidelity, visual–narration alignment, timeline plan
realization, long-horizon consistency, and end-to-end delivery success.

3 WeVisCut Agent Framework

Figure 2 presents the WeVisCut agent framework for long-form
script-to-video production. A shared policy makes editing decisions, while a
stateful runtime executes tools and maintains the resulting production
artifacts. Their interaction connects the narration script to a rendered video
through an inspectable process that supports revision when execution reveals
problems. We first define the task and policy–runtime interface, then describe
the harness that implements this process.

3.1 Script-to-Video Task

A task input $x$ consists of a fixed narration script $S$ , target aspect ratio $a^{\star}$ , feasible duration interval $D^{\star}$ , optional source assets, and a
versioned shared tool space $\mathcal{T}$ . The goal is to render a video $V$ satisfying the delivery constraints without rewriting the narration. The
outputs also include a typed shot plan $P$ , specifying narration coverage,
visual content, and timing, and an execution trajectory $\tau$ recording calls
and returns throughout the production process (Appendix C ).

Production uses tools that accept typed arguments and return artifacts or
execution feedback. At call $t$ , a single policy $\pi_{\theta}$ with shared
parameters $\theta$ serves a role $r_{t}\in\{\mathrm{O},\mathrm{G},\mathrm{C}\}$ .
The orchestrator ( $\mathrm{O}$ ) selects tools and arguments, the generator
( $\mathrm{G}$ ) supplies structured content, and the critic ( $\mathrm{C}$ )
inspects artifacts and requests or verifies repairs based on recorded execution feedback.

At each call, the role context $c_{t}$ combines the task input, role, a view of
the production state $s_{t}$ (shot plan, artifacts, and progress), relevant
call-and-return history $h_{t}$ , and tool schemas. The policy produces a complete
role output $z_{t}$ , which the runtime $\mathcal{E}$ processes under a tool contract $u_{t}$ :

$z_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}^{(r_{t})}(\cdot\mid c_{t}),\qquad(o_{t},s_{t+1})\sim\mathcal{E}_{r_{t},u_{t}}(\cdot\mid s_{t},z_{t}).$ (1)

The contract specifies the tool, required fields, and value types. The runtime
returns observation $o_{t}$ , reporting results or blockers, and the updated
production state $s_{t+1}$ . For orchestrator calls, the role output $z_{t}$ specifies a tool action $a_{t}$ containing the selected tool and arguments
(Figure 2 ).

Figure 2: The WeVisCut agent harness with stateful tool selection. A shared policy chooses legal
tools from the evolving production state. Validated returns update
artifacts and inform inspection, local repair, and replanning before
final stitching and delivery.

3.2 Harness Design

The harness in Figure 2 implements the interaction in
Equation 1 through a shared tool space, persistent production
state, and runtime checks. Each orchestrator turn selects one tool, which may
process multiple shots and invoke generator or critic calls. Tool selection
depends on the current production state, including available artifacts and blockers,
rather than a predetermined sequence.

Production flow and tool system. Starting from the narration script $S$ , segmentation produces production
segments, and shot planning expands them into the shot plan $P$ . Shot records
connect narration spans to visual queries, target durations, and spotlight
assignments. Visual retrieval and text-to-speech (TTS) tools use these records
to obtain shot assets and narration audio. For spotlight shots, decoration
tools select verified HyperFrames templates [ 7 ] and
fill typed slots, allowing the policy to control presentation through
structured content rather than raw layout code. Music search supplies
background music (BGM) for the final mix. Timeline composition combines shot
assets, narration audio, and decorations, while final rendering produces the
shot videos required for stitching. Quality judging and repair tools allow
the policy to revisit these intermediate products before delivery. This
progression describes artifact dependencies, not an obligatory ordering of
every tool call.

Persistent state and selective context. These tools communicate through the production state $s_{t}$ . Global records
track narration coverage, shot order, and timing, while per-shot records
associate assets, audio, templates, renders, and unresolved issues with shot
IDs. Planned content remains distinct from artifacts that have actually been
produced. Episode memory retains these artifacts, and the event trace
preserves calls, returns, and failures. The initial observation exposes the
full production state. Subsequent role contexts $c_{t}$ contain the latest
runtime observation, state changes, a progress summary, and relevant local
records. Separating persistent storage from model context avoids repeatedly
serializing the entire project while preserving the information needed for
later inspection and repair.

Validated execution and actionable feedback. Given a tool action $a_{t}$ , the runtime identifies the tool contract $u_{t}$ and
checks argument schemas, artifact references, and execution prerequisites.
When structured content or inspection is required, it supplies this contract
in the generator’s or critic’s role context. Only the runtime commits
validated results to the production state, whereas
rejected calls return typed blockers that identify what must be corrected or
produced next. For example, a stitching request cannot substitute for missing
shot renders or background music. The runtime reports these missing
prerequisites, allowing the orchestrator to select the necessary production
tools before retrying. This separation keeps model decisions grounded in
execution evidence rather than treating a proposed plan as completed work.

Artifact-grounded inspection and bounded repair. Execution feedback also determines when revision is needed. Quality inspection
combines structured checks of shot records, timing, and render status with a
VLM-Judge over spotlight renders. The critic identifies affected shots and the
defects that a repair should address. Repair tools then revise the relevant
content and recheck affected downstream artifacts while retaining still-valid
results. For example, correcting a decoration need not regenerate unchanged
narration audio. Repair limits bound repeated correction attempts, but
exhausting this budget does not remove the artifact prerequisites for delivery.
Quality warnings and missing production artifacts therefore remain distinct
conditions. The representative trajectory in Appendix D illustrates how inspection, blocked calls, and targeted follow-up actions
interact within this loop.

Verified delivery and trace retention. When the required artifacts are available, the policy requests final
stitching. Reaching the call limit also triggers a delivery attempt from the
available production state. Before stitching, the runtime checks the required
shot renders, narration audio, and background music. After stitching, it
validates the video and delivery manifest. Missing prerequisites are returned
as blockers while further calls are available. A run that terminates without a
validated final video is recorded as incomplete, with its execution trace
retained for diagnosis. The resulting production records support executable
data construction in Section 4 and policy training in
Section 5 . This production harness is separate from the
read-only evaluation harness in Appendix B.2 .

4 Data Construction

The harness records execution, but finished videos expose only narration
and shot structure, not the tool calls, feedback, and repairs that produced
them. To train the shared policy, we recover editing intent and replay it
under the same state and action contract, constructing role-specific
examples grounded in real tool returns from executing the recovered intent.

Our source data consists of finished narration-driven videos collected from
Bilibili, spanning technology and digital products, business and finance,
natural science, and social sciences and humanities. The admitted set contains
3,750 video projects.

4.1 Executable Trajectory Construction

To convert these videos into executable supervision, we use a four-stage
pipeline comprising editing inversion, verified replay, observe–reflect–repair,
and trajectory materialization (Figure 3 ).

Figure 3: Executable data construction through editing
inversion, verified replay, observe–reflect–repair, and trajectory
materialization. (A) Editing inversion recovers aligned narration and shot
evidence from a finished video. (B) Verified replay executes this intent
with deployment-aligned production tools. (C) Observe–reflect–repair
performs bounded inspection and targeted repair to preserve successful
artifacts. (D) Trajectory materialization organizes recorded tool
interactions, feedback, and repair links into role-specific training
examples and rollout records.

Editing Inversion. We first recover script–shot intent from the source videos
(Figure 3 A), including timestamped narration, shot structure, and
complete sentence–shot alignment for every admitted project. Narration
provides temporal anchors, while aligned shots indicate how the content is
organized visually. Together, this evidence forms a structured storyboard
describing what each shot should communicate, how long it should last, and
which production constraints it should satisfy. This process recovers the
intended video structure, not the original editor’s tool actions.

Verified Replay. The recovered storyboard specifies what to produce, but does not provide
execution feedback. Verified replay (Figure 3 B) therefore executes
this intent through a production toolchain aligned with the deployment state
and action contract.
Schema-checked tools maintain a shared production state across planning
(article segmentation, shot planning, and self-reflection), asset production
(visual retrieval with TTS, spotlight decoration, music search, and severity
ranking), and timeline execution (composition with judging, final rendering,
and stitching). Each real tool
return updates this state before the next decision. The runtime verifies
asset references, audio durations, template inputs, timeline positions, and
rendered outputs against the artifacts actually produced. Spotlight shots use
verified HyperFrames templates [ 7 ] that separate
layout, motion, and visual style. The policy selects a template and fills
typed slots instead of generating raw HTML, CSS, or JavaScript.

Observe–Reflect–Repair. During replay, quality inspection combines structured shot, timing,
decoration-slot, and render-status checks with a VLM-Judge over spotlight
renders. Using these inspection results, we locate production problems,
apply bounded local repairs supported by the toolchain, and verify these
changes while preserving successful artifacts (Figure 3 C).

Trajectory Materialization. These executions produce trajectories that retain pre-action states, tool
names and arguments, real returns, created artifacts, quality verdicts, and
failure–repair links. Missing arguments, unresolved references, invalid stage
order, and failed delivery checks remain visible rather than being rewritten
as successes. We then organize these trajectories into role-specific examples
and rollout records (Figure 3 D).

4.2 Training Examples and Data Split

These examples provide role-specific supervision under the framework in
Figure 2 . Orchestrator samples supervise tool selection and
structured arguments, while generator samples cover script segments, shot
plans, retrieval queries, and template content. Critic samples provide failure
localization, repair requests, and verification decisions. Runtime
observations remain context, with loss applied only to model-authored tokens.

We partition by source project, keeping all its examples and repair
records together. Held-out projects supply the benchmark in
Section 6.1 and are excluded from training. The
134,771 examples supervise a single Qwen3.5-9B policy with shared production
state and tool vocabulary across all three roles.

5 Training WeVisCut

The executable examples provide initial supervision, but WeVisCut also needs
to handle the states produced by its own decisions. Figure 4 summarizes three training phases, comprising unified SFT and runtime-verified
RFT, recovery learning with Guided-OPD, and decision optimization with RCF-DAPO . The policy interaction and agent harness are defined in
Section 3 .

Figure 4: Three training phases of WeVisCut. Phase 1 learns tool use and
recovery through unified SFT and runtime-verified RFT. Phase 2 applies
Guided-OPD with decreasing teacher intervention. Phase 3 uses RCF-DAPO to compare alternatives within matched roles and decision
contexts.

5.1 Phase 1: Unified SFT and RFT

We first train Qwen3.5-9B with LoRA [ 8 ] on the 134,771
examples constructed above. Following AgentTuning and
Agent-FLAN [ 46 , 2 ] , we supervise model
responses while retaining tool feedback as input.

Let $\mathcal{D}_{\rm SFT}$ contain $N_{\rm SFT}$ training examples.
We index examples by $e$ and tokens within an example by $j$ .
The conversation $y_{e}=(y_{e,1},\ldots,y_{e,L_{e}})$ has $L_{e}$ tokens,
and $y_{e,<j}$ is the prefix before position $j$ . Before each response,
this prefix encodes the role context $c_{t}$ , including instructions and
available tool feedback. The loss mask $m_{e,j}$ is one for tokens in the
target model response and zero for instructions, tool-return messages, and
padding tokens added to equalize sequence lengths for batching. Thus only
response tokens contribute to the supervised fine-tuning loss

$\mathcal{L}_{\rm SFT}=-\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{e=1}^{N_{\rm SFT}}\sum_{j=1}^{L_{e}}m_{e,j}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{e,j}\mid y_{e,<j})}{\displaystyle\sum_{e=1}^{N_{\rm SFT}}\sum_{j=1}^{L_{e}}m_{e,j}}.$ (2)

The denominator counts the supervised tokens, so the loss averages their
negative log probabilities across all supervised model responses in the corpus.

SFT teaches the recorded production steps, but the student can encounter
new failures when executing tasks on its own. We collect additional
supervision through runtime-verified rejection-sampled fine-tuning (RFT).
At the first recoverable failure, we restore the last replayable state before
the error and keep the valid earlier steps. The teacher regenerates the
complete remaining sequence from that state. Both student-only successes and
teacher-assisted repairs are replayed and admitted only if they pass the
runtime checks and a quality threshold selected on development tasks.
We track and balance these two sources of supervision. Failures that cannot
be restored enter a bounded pool for later rollout sampling.
Appendix A.1 gives the admission rules for both sources of supervision.

5.2 Phase 2: Recovery Learning with Guided-OPD

We next apply Guided-OPD [ 12 ] to learn decisions while interacting
with the production environment. At training update $n$ , the teacher
generates a complete role output with probability $\beta_{n}$ , and the
student generates it with probability $1-\beta_{n}$ . At call $t$ , we draw $q_{t}\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(\beta_{n})$ and use

$z_{t}\sim\begin{cases}\pi_{\phi}^{(r_{t})}(\cdot\mid c_{t}),&q_{t}=1,\\
\pi_{\theta}^{(r_{t})}(\cdot\mid c_{t}),&q_{t}=0\end{cases}.$ (3)

Here $\phi$ is the frozen teacher’s parameter set. Both policies receive
the same role context $c_{t}$ . As in Equation 1 , the complete
output $z_{t}$ is executed before the next call, when control is sampled again.

Both branches train the student. Teacher outputs use forward KL against
the teacher distribution renormalized over its $K_{\rm vocab}$ most likely
tokens, reducing the cost of distillation. Student outputs use reverse KL
on their own generated prefixes. We gradually reduce $\beta_{n}$ from a
value below one to zero, so training ends with fully student-controlled
executions. Appendix A.1 specifies the token masks, KL losses,
and intervention schedule used throughout this training phase.

5.3 Phase 3: Decision Optimization with RCF-DAPO

Finally, we compare alternative outputs for the same production decision.
Our Role- and Context-Factored DAPO ( RCF-DAPO ) adapts
DAPO [ 43 ] by restricting reward comparisons to the same role
and production context. At a fixed role call, we sample candidate outputs $z_{i}$ , where $i$ indexes alternatives in the comparison group. For example,
generator candidates may be different retrieval queries for the same shot.
For orchestrator calls, we restore the
same production state, sample alternative next actions, and execute each
with a frozen continuation policy, the same tool registry, budget, and
paired random seeds. Generator and critic outputs are evaluated under the
same local request, artifact, and tool contract.

Each candidate receives a role-specific reward $R_{i}^{(r)}$ .
Orchestrator rewards combine successful delivery and final quality with
partial credit for persistent progress on failed executions. Generator
rewards assess schema validity, requirement coverage, and content usability.
Critic rewards assess diagnosis, repair utility, and verification, with a
penalty for unnecessary repairs. The scoring rules, rubrics, and weights
are fixed. Appendix A.2 defines the complete reward formulas.

Within a group with matching conditions $\kappa$ , we convert rewards into
relative advantages

$A_{i}^{(r)}=\operatorname{sg}\!\left[\frac{R_{i}^{(r)}-\mu_{\kappa}^{(r)}}{\sigma_{\kappa}^{(r)}+\epsilon}\right],$ (4)

where $\mu_{\kappa}^{(r)}$ and $\sigma_{\kappa}^{(r)}$ are the group’s mean
reward and standard deviation, $\epsilon>0$ prevents division by zero,
and $\operatorname{sg}$ stops gradients through the advantages.
Above-mean rewards yield positive advantages, and below-mean rewards yield
negative ones. Groups with insufficient
reward variation are resampled, with quotas and a replay queue maintaining
coverage. Contexts are collected from the current policy with gradients
stopped, and role losses are balanced after token-level normalization.
We retain DAPO’s asymmetric Clip-Higher and soft overlong shaping.
Appendix A.2 details the sampling and updates.

6 Experiments

We evaluate whether task-specific training improves script-to-video production
and whether these gains extend to complete editing systems. We first compare
policies in a shared tool environment, then compare WeVisCut with editors using
their native workflows. Separating these comparisons distinguishes policy
performance from differences in tools and orchestration. We further examine
the cumulative training stages and assess the rendered videos through blinded
human evaluation.

6.1 Experimental Setup

Benchmark and implementation. We construct a fixed benchmark of 100 script-to-video tasks from the held-out
source projects in Section 4 . Each task specifies a
narration script, source pool, target duration, and aspect ratio. All systems
receive identical task inputs and delivery constraints. Our
trainable variants share the Qwen3.5-9B backbone, training split, decoding
configuration, tool registry, and rendering budget. The Guided-OPD and RCF-DAPO training configurations are given in Appendix
Table 5 .

Comparison methods. For the policy comparison, all models use the same instructions, tool schemas,
runtime, and rendering budget. We evaluate Qwen3.5-9B, Qwen3.6-27B, and
Qwen3.6-35B-A3B [ 24 , 25 , 26 ] , together with
Qwen3.5-397B-A17B, Gemini 3.1 Pro [ 5 ] , and a fixed
Claude Opus 5 snapshot. These reference models receive no task-specific
post-training. Our variants differ only in the training stages applied to the
shared 9B backbone within the same production environment.

We compare WeVisCut with Crayotter [ 38 ] and the
commentary-capable VideoAgent workflow [ 49 ] , each using
its released tools and orchestration. Adapters may translate file formats and
export evaluation artifacts, but may not retrieve assets, write narration,
create plans, or repair outputs. CutClaw, CineAgents, and
DirectorAgent [ 48 , 47 , 21 ] are
excluded from this quantitative comparison because their native workflows
omit or replace parts of the fixed-narration, fixed-source task contract.
Appendix Table 6 shows whether each system
supports the required inputs, shot plans, and execution.

Evaluation metrics. We propose five complementary metrics for script-to-video production that
assess the relationship between the source script, editing plan, execution
trace, and rendered video. All scores lie in $[0,100]$ , with higher values
indicating better performance. Script Content Fidelity (SCF) measures
the coverage, faithfulness, and ordering of narration claims relative to the
source script. Visual–Narration Semantic Match (VNSM) assesses whether
rendered visuals support the concurrent narration. Timeline Plan Realization
(TPR) compares planned and realized shots in presence, duration, and order.
Long-Horizon Consistency (LHC) measures late-stage shot-health retention and
asset diversity. End-to-End Rendering Yield (ERY) is the fraction of held-out
tasks producing a decodable, sufficiently complete video with the required
non-silent narration. Overall Score is the equally weighted mean of the five
reported metric means, used only as a descriptive summary
for comparing aggregate task performance.

Production Cost Efficiency (PCE) is reported separately as tool calls and
generated tokens per successful output minute. For native systems, we instead
report wall-clock minutes and estimated USD per successful output minute,
since the underlying tools and services differ. Both measures include failed
attempts in the cost numerator, and lower values are better. Definitions,
eligibility rules, and missing-data treatment are given in
Appendix B .

Evaluation protocol. Each system is evaluated in five independent runs over the 100 tasks. We
report across-run means and standard deviations for the five component
metrics, with no uncertainty estimate for Overall Score. Permissions and
retry limits are fixed within the shared environment, and thresholds,
adapters, and evaluator settings are selected on development tasks.
Semantic evaluation uses timestamped contact sheets in a read-only,
network-free environment with access restricted to metric-specific evidence.
The held-out evaluator, its prompts, and the five reported metrics are not
used as post-SFT training signals. The metric-validation protocol is described
in Appendix B.1 , and evidence collection and
isolation within the read-only evaluation environment are detailed in
Appendix B.2 .

7 Conclusion

We presented WeVisCut, a system for long-form script-to-video production built
around a single 9B policy shared by the orchestrator, generator, and critic.
Our four-stage data pipeline combines editing inversion and verified replay
to recover executable supervision from finished videos. Training progresses
from executable imitation to recovery learning and reward-based decision
optimization. Our 100-task benchmark connects source scripts, editing plans,
execution traces, and final videos through five complementary metrics.
The trained policy improves all five metrics over its base model and achieves
mean scores close to those of Claude Opus 5 while using approximately one thirty-third
as many generated tokens per successful output minute. Native-system
comparisons and blinded human ratings also favor WeVisCut. Broader template
coverage and more robust retrieval are needed to extend the system to a wider
range of editing tasks.

Appendix A Optimization and Training Details

We use the notation of Sections 3 and 5 throughout: $t$ indexes role calls, $j$ indexes tokens, and $n$ indexes training updates. The task input is $x$ ,
and $\pi_{\theta}^{(r)}$ is the shared policy conditioned on role $r$ .

A.1 Executable RFT and Guided-OPD

Replay-safe RFT. Let $t^{\star}$ index the call at which the first runtime failure occurs.
A failure is recoverable when
the environment can restore the latest transaction snapshot before $t^{\star}$ . External side effects are executed in a sandbox or through
idempotent calls. We retain the valid prefix, provide the teacher with the
failure record and restored state, and regenerate the complete suffix.
The repaired trajectory is retained only after replay from the snapshot
passes schema, prerequisite, artifact-reference, render, duration, narration,
lint, and black-frame checks. Its quality score $Q_{\rm RFT}$ also needs to
meet the development-selected minimum $q_{\rm RFT}$ : $Q_{\rm RFT}\geq q_{\rm RFT}$ . Complete student successes pass the same
admission checks. Student-only and teacher-assisted records have separate
provenance tags and fixed source–task quotas. Unrecoverable cases are kept
in a class-balanced, capped hard-case pool for later rollout sampling,
with fixed mixing probability $\eta$ .

Turn-level intervention and loss routing. Following Guided-OPD [ 12 ] , call $t$ consists of a complete role
output $z_{t}$ followed by its runtime observation $o_{t}$ . At update $n$ , $q_{t}\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(\beta_{n})$ selects the teacher when $q_{t}=1$ and the student otherwise. The selected output is executed before control
is sampled again. Both branches contribute distillation loss. We denote
the mixed rollout distribution by $\mu_{n}=\pi_{\theta,\phi}^{\beta_{n}}$ ,
where $\theta$ and $\phi$ are the student and frozen teacher parameters.

The models share a tokenizer. For teacher outputs, the teacher distribution
is renormalized over its $K_{\rm vocab}$ most likely tokens, giving $\widetilde{\pi}_{\phi}^{K_{\rm vocab}}$ . Student outputs use reverse KL, while
teacher outputs use this truncated forward KL:

$\mathcal{L}_{\rm OPD}=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mu_{n}}\!\left[\frac{1}{N_{\tau}}\sum_{t,j}m_{t,j}\left((1-q_{t})D_{\rm KL}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\phi})+q_{t}D_{\rm KL}(\widetilde{\pi}_{\phi}^{K_{\rm vocab}}\|\pi_{\theta})\right)_{c_{t,j}}\right].$ (5)

Here $c_{t,j}$ contains the role context $c_{t}$ and all tokens preceding
position $j$ in call $t$ . The policies in each KL term are conditioned on
this token prefix. The mask $m_{t,j}$ is one for assistant tokens and zero
for input, padding, and runtime observations, and $N_{\tau}=\sum_{t,j}m_{t,j}>0$ counts supervised tokens.
Thus teacher-only trajectories also train the student. We log retained
top- $K_{\rm vocab}$ probability mass to audit truncation. This is an
efficiency variant of Guided-OPD. Step-Level OPD instead keeps trajectories
student-controlled and queries teacher targets afterward [ 33 ] .

Teacher intervention follows

$\beta_{n}=\beta_{\rm start}\frac{1+\cos(\pi\min(n/T_{\rm decay},1))}{2},\qquad\beta_{\rm start}=0.8,$ (6)

where $T_{\rm decay}$ is the number of updates over which intervention
decays, set to 70% of training. The remaining 30% uses student-only
rollouts. Only checkpoints evaluated without teacher intervention proceed
to RL. Guided-OPD uses a 50-call horizon with early termination, while RL
uses the 16-call deployment budget. Token limits are per-call
context/response caps, and compact state updates limit history growth.

A.2 Role- and Context-Factored DAPO

Optimization scope. Each trainable call records its role $r$ , context $c$ , output $z$ , observation $o$ , and execution contract $u$ . Main rollouts populate a snapshot buffer.
For an orchestrator context $c_{t}$ , we restore the pre-decision state,
sample alternative outputs, and execute each with the same frozen
continuation policy $\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}$ , budget, and seed set.
Here $\theta_{\rm old}$ is the policy parameter snapshot frozen for the
current optimization iteration.
Nested generator and critic tokens are masked from the orchestrator loss.
Generator and critic contexts are also collected from current-policy
rollouts and resampled locally. Let $d_{r}$ be the context distribution for
role $r$ and $\lambda_{r}$ its outer loss weight. The optimized proxy is

$J_{\rm proxy}=\sum_{r\in\{\rm O,G,C\}}\lambda_{r}\mathbb{E}_{c\sim\operatorname{sg}(d_{r}),z\sim\pi_{\theta}^{(r)}(\cdot\mid c)}[R^{(r)}(c,z)].$ (7)

Here $R^{(r)}(c,z)$ is the expected execution reward for output $z$ in
context $c$ , with runtime observations averaged out.
This is a conditional call-level objective. Frozen continuations and the
sampled context distribution remain part of the proxy, so it does not
provide an unbiased gradient of the terminal production return.

Matched comparison groups. For orchestrator call $t$ , we fix $\kappa_{\rm O,t}=(x,c_{t},s_{t},\mathcal{T},B_{t},\pi_{\rm cont})$ :
the task input, context, restored state, tool space, remaining budget $B_{t}$ ,
and continuation policy $\pi_{\rm cont}=\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}$ .
Only the next output varies. Generator groups fix the local request,
artifact, tool, schema/version, budget, and initial state.
Critic groups also fix the defect and verification contract. Candidate
executions share paired seeds. Let $\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}$ be the group of
outputs sampled under matching conditions $\kappa$ .

Features are mapped to their registered ranges: $[0,1]$ , except repair
utility in $[-1,1]$ . Fixed weights produce one reward per output before
group normalization. Contexts are sampled through role–task–tool quotas.
A group is accepted only if $\operatorname{Var}_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}}(R_{i}^{(r)})>\delta$ ,
where $\delta$ is the minimum reward-variance threshold. Otherwise it is
resampled up to a fixed cap. Low-variance contexts remain in a coverage
queue and are revisited through executable imitation. For an accepted group,

$A_{i}^{(r)}=\operatorname{sg}\!\left[\frac{R_{i}^{(r)}-\mu_{\kappa}^{(r)}}{\sigma_{\kappa}^{(r)}+\epsilon}\right],\qquad\mu_{\kappa}^{(r)}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}}R_{i}^{(r)}.$ (8)

Here $\sigma_{\kappa}^{(r)}$ is the group reward standard deviation, $\epsilon>0$ stabilizes normalization, and $\operatorname{sg}$ stops
gradients through group statistics. No normalization mixes roles or
unmatched contexts. Resampling emphasizes informative reward differences
and changes the sampled context distribution.

Role-specific rewards. For candidate $i$ , let $z_{i}$ be its role output and $o_{i}$ its execution
feedback. An orchestrator output is scored by continuing from snapshot $t$ .
We measure progress through validated milestones:

$\displaystyle\Phi(s)$ $\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k}\omega_{k}I_{k}(s)}{\sum_{k}\omega_{k}},\qquad r_{\ell}^{\rm prog}=\gamma\Phi(s_{\ell+1})-\Phi(s_{\ell}),$ $\displaystyle F_{i,t}^{\rightarrow}$ $\displaystyle=(1-\epsilon_{p})\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\sum_{\ell\geq t}\gamma^{\ell-t}r_{\ell}^{\rm prog},0,1\right).$ (9)

Here $I_{k}(s)\in\{0,1\}$ indicates whether unique milestone $k$ holds in
state $s$ , $\omega_{k}>0$ is its fixed weight, $0<\gamma\leq 1$ is the discount
factor, and $\ell$ indexes subsequent calls until branch $i$ terminates. Repetition adds no
milestone credit, and later invalidation removes it. The positive margin $0<\epsilon_{p}<1$ keeps failed-branch progress below the delivery tier.
The operator $\operatorname{clip}(\cdot,0,1)$ bounds its argument to $[0,1]$ .

The rewards are

$\displaystyle R_{i}^{\mathrm{O}}$ $\displaystyle=g_{{\rm del},i}[\alpha+(1-\alpha)Q_{{\rm end},i}]+(1-g_{{\rm del},i})\alpha F_{i,t}^{\rightarrow}-\lambda_{p}p_{i},$ $\displaystyle R_{i}^{\mathrm{G}}$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{k}w_{k}^{\mathrm{G}}f_{k}^{\mathrm{G}}(c,z_{i},o_{i}),$ $\displaystyle R_{i}^{\mathrm{C}}$ $\displaystyle=w_{d}f_{{\rm diag},i}+w_{r}U_{\rm repair}(d_{i})+w_{v}f_{{\rm verify},i}-w_{u}f_{{\rm unnecessary},i}.$ (10)

The delivery indicator $g_{{\rm del},i}$ is one only if termination, schema,
prerequisite, artifact, and loop checks pass and final quality $Q_{{\rm end},i}\geq q_{\min}$ , where $q_{\min}$ is the minimum delivery
quality. Otherwise it is zero. A legal stop below this threshold is
premature, recorded by the indicator $p_{i}\in\{0,1\}$ .
The parameter $\alpha\in[0,1]$ weights delivery and progress credit
relative to final quality, and $\lambda_{p}\geq 0$ weights the premature-stop penalty.

Final quality combines a rule score and a sampled render audit: $Q_{\rm end}=(1-w_{a})Q_{\rm rule}+w_{a}Q_{\rm audit}$ ,
where $Q_{\rm rule},Q_{\rm audit}\in[0,1]$ and $w_{a}\in[0,1]$ is the fixed
audit weight. The six rule features are plan
coverage, spotlight-decoration coverage, inspection coverage, plan hygiene,
completion, and efficiency, weighted 0.353, 0.186, 0.179, 0.144, 0.081, and
0.057. The render audit scores visual impact and clarity. Thus these six
weights apply to rule features, with the audit blended separately.

Generator features $f_{k}^{\mathrm{G}}$ measure schema validity, grounding,
requirement coverage, and downstream usability using deterministic checks
and a frozen tool-specific rubric. All features are set to zero for an
invalid schema. Critic features $f_{{\rm diag},i}$ and $f_{{\rm verify},i}$ score diagnosis and verification,
and $f_{{\rm unnecessary},i}$ penalizes unnecessary repair.
The coefficients $w_{k}^{\mathrm{G}},w_{d},w_{r},w_{v},w_{u}$ are fixed feature weights.
Here $d_{i}$ is the diagnosis provided by critic output $z_{i}$ . For a diagnosis $d$ , controlled repair utility is

$U_{\rm repair}(d)=\frac{1}{|\Xi|}\sum_{\xi\in\Xi}\left[Q(A_{\rm fix}(d;\xi))-Q(A_{\rm fix}(d_{0};\xi))\right],$ (11)

where $A_{\rm fix}$ is a frozen repair executor with identical tool version
and budget, $d_{0}$ is an empty diagnosis, $\Xi$ is a paired seed set, and $Q$ maps repair results to a fixed quality score in $[0,1]$ . Thus utility measures the
improvement over the empty-diagnosis baseline under controlled execution.
The training judge, rubric, prompt, and version hash are frozen and
independent of the held-out evaluator. Rule and judge scores are logged
separately.

DAPO update. Let $\mathcal{B}_{r}$ be the candidate batch for role $r$ , and $\mathcal{M}_{i}^{(r)}$ the model-authored token positions in output $z_{i}$ .
For token $j$ , the policy probability ratio is $\rho_{i,j}=\pi_{\theta}(z_{i,j}\mid c_{i},z_{i,<j})/\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(z_{i,j}\mid c_{i},z_{i,<j})$ ,
where $c_{i}$ is the candidate’s input context.
We use DAPO’s asymmetric Clip-Higher interval [ 43 ] , with $\varepsilon_{\rm low}=0.20$ and $\varepsilon_{\rm high}=0.28$ :

$\displaystyle\ell_{i,j}^{(r)}$ $\displaystyle=\min\!\left(\rho_{i,j}A_{i}^{(r)},\operatorname{clip}(\rho_{i,j},0.8,1.28)A_{i}^{(r)}\right),$ $\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\rm RCF\text{-}DAPO}$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{r}\lambda_{r}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{r}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}_{i}^{(r)}}\ell_{i,j}^{(r)}}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{r}}|\mathcal{M}_{i}^{(r)}|}\right].$ (12)

Token losses are aggregated within each role before applying the outer
weights $\lambda_{\rm O}=\lambda_{\rm G}=\lambda_{\rm C}=1/3$ .
The old policy is synchronized once per optimization iteration.
We use neither dual clipping nor a loss-side KL term.

For generated token count $L$ , let $L_{\rm soft}<L_{\rm cap}$ denote the
no-penalty threshold and generation hard cap. For non-truncated responses,
soft overlong shaping is

$p_{\rm len}(L)=\begin{cases}0,&L\leq L_{\rm soft},\\
-\lambda_{\rm len}\dfrac{L-L_{\rm soft}}{L_{\rm cap}-L_{\rm soft}},&L_{\rm soft}<L<L_{\rm cap}.\end{cases}$ (13)

We set $L_{\rm soft}=L_{\rm cap}-2{,}048$ and the length-penalty weight $\lambda_{\rm len}=0.1$ .
Responses reaching the hard cap are replaced before an eight-candidate
group is formed. If the resampling cap is reached first, the group is
dropped. Truncated responses enter neither rewards, group statistics,
nor policy loss. Role-wise gradient norms and pairwise cosine similarities
are logged, since outer loss weights control scale but do not eliminate
shared-parameter gradient conflict.

Appendix B Metrics and Evidence Protocol

The five component metrics lie in $[0,100]$ and are reported individually,
alongside the descriptive Overall Score defined below. Component claims, shot
IDs, matching rules, thresholds, and denominators are frozen before test
outputs are inspected.

Script Content Fidelity (SCF). Let $c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}$ be the claims in source script $S$ , and $s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}$ the claims in output narration $\mathcal{V}$ .
The entailment indicator $E(a,B)\in\{0,1\}$ tests whether text $B$ supports claim $a$ ,
and $\tau_{K}$ is the Kendall order correlation of matched source and output claims.
We compute

$\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}$ $\displaystyle=m^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}E(c_{i},\mathcal{V}),$ $\displaystyle\mathrm{Faith}$ $\displaystyle=k^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}E(s_{j},S),$ $\displaystyle\mathrm{Ord}$ $\displaystyle=(1+\tau_{K})/2,$ $\displaystyle\mathrm{SCF}$ $\displaystyle=100(0.40\mathrm{Cov}+0.35\mathrm{Faith}+0.25\mathrm{Ord}).$ (14)

SCF is valid for at least five source claims and one narration unit.

Visual–Narration Semantic Match (VNSM). Let $\mathcal{S}$ contain the registered shots with a valid evaluator response.
For each shot $s$ , four uniformly sampled frames form montage $F_{s}$ , paired
with concurrent narration $T_{s}$ . The held-out Claude Opus 5 evaluator assigns
an anchored grade $g_{s}=g(F_{s},T_{s})\in\{1,2,3,4\}$ (Poor, Fair, Good,
Excellent) using the rubric below. We rescale grades to $[0,100]$ and aggregate

$\mathrm{VNSM}=|\mathcal{S}|^{-1}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{100(g_{s}-1)}{3},\qquad|\mathcal{S}|\geq 5.$ (15)

Timeline Plan Realization (TPR). Planned and realized shots are joined by frozen shot ID:

$\displaystyle R_{\rm pres}$ $\displaystyle=|P\cap A|/|P|,$ $\displaystyle R_{\rm dur}$ $\displaystyle=|P\cap A|^{-1}\!\sum_{s\in P\cap A}\exp\!\left[-\frac{|D_{s}^{\rm plan}-D_{s}^{\rm act}|}{\max(0.5,0.3D_{s}^{\rm plan})}\right],$ $\displaystyle R_{\rm ord}$ $\displaystyle=\mathrm{LCS}(P,A)/\max(|P|,|A|),$ $\displaystyle\mathrm{TPR}$ $\displaystyle=100(0.30R_{\rm pres}+0.40R_{\rm dur}+0.30R_{\rm ord}).$ (16)

We set $R_{\rm dur}=0$ when $P\cap A=\varnothing$ .

Long-Horizon Consistency (LHC). Shot health averages render success, audio–clip duration agreement, and the
rescaled VNSM grade:

$q_{s}=\frac{1}{3}\!\left[\mathbf{1}(s\text{ rendered})+\exp\!\left(-\frac{|D_{s}^{\rm audio}-D_{s}^{\rm clip}|}{\max(0.5,0.3D_{s}^{\rm audio})}\right)+\frac{g_{s}-1}{3}\right].$ (17)

Unavailable $g_{s}$ is omitted and the observable terms are renormalized. For
the first and last thirds $H,T$ and canonical asset digests $\mathcal{A}$ ,

$\displaystyle R_{\rm ret}$ $\displaystyle=\operatorname{clip}(\bar{q}_{T}/\bar{q}_{H},0,1),$ $\displaystyle R_{\rm nov}$ $\displaystyle=|\operatorname{unique}(\mathcal{A})|/|\mathcal{A}|,$ $\displaystyle\mathrm{LHC}$ $\displaystyle=100(0.60R_{\rm ret}+0.40R_{\rm nov}).$ (18)

LHC requires $N\geq 12$ and $\bar{q}_{H}\geq 0.5$ .

End-to-End Rendering Yield (ERY).

$\mathrm{ERY}=\frac{100}{N_{\rm total}}\sum_{c}\mathbf{1}[\mathrm{ok}(c)],\quad\mathrm{ok}=\mathrm{exists}\wedge\mathrm{decodable}\wedge[D^{\rm act}\geq 0.5D^{\rm plan}]\wedge\mathrm{hasNarration}.$ (19)

hasNarration requires non-silent speech covering at least half of the
planned narration duration. Background music does not qualify. Timeouts,
crashes, and unstarted tasks remain in the denominator.

Overall Score (descriptive summary). For each model or system within a comparison track, let $\overline{M}_{k}$ denote its reported mean for component $k$ . We add a post-hoc descriptive
summary of these means:

$\mathrm{Overall}=\frac{1}{5}\sum_{k\in\{\mathrm{SCF,VNSM,TPR,LHC,ERY}\}}\overline{M}_{k}.$ (20)

Each component receives weight $0.2$ . These weights are not fitted to model
rankings or human preferences. The shared $[0,100]$ scale makes this a transparent
macro-average, but does not make the components independent or equally
calibrated: LHC already incorporates VNSM in shot health. Overall therefore
summarizes task performance rather than serving as a separately validated
measure of perceptual video quality. It is distinct from the human-rated
overall preference in Table 4 .

The displayed scores are computed from the reported, rounded component means
and rounded to one decimal place. Each component retains its own eligibility
rules and denominator. Overall does not imply a common per-task population.
If any component mean is unavailable, Overall is N/A rather than an average
over the remaining metrics. Costs are excluded. We report no Overall standard
deviation, confidence interval, or significance test: these require aligned
run-level results or the cross-metric covariance, which cannot be recovered
from the component standard deviations alone. All five components remain
visible so that the summary does not conceal trade-offs.

Production Cost Efficiency (PCE). For tool calls or generated tokens $X$ ,

$\mathrm{PCE}_{X}=\frac{\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}_{\rm all}}X(c)}{\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}_{\rm ok}}D_{c}^{\rm act}/60}.$ (21)

PCE is lower-is-better and is never combined with quality. Eligibility counts
and validity conditions are recorded alongside metric reporting. Conditional
means use valid tasks, whereas unconditional means assign zero to system
failures. Harness or provider outages remain missing rather than being
converted into low-quality scores.

B.1 Diagnostic Validation

We apply three strengths of metric-targeted corruption: claim edits (SCF),
time-local visual substitution (VNSM), shot removal/duration/order changes
(TPR), tail degradation or asset repetition (LHC), and delivery failure (ERY).
For each metric–corruption pair we report the paired drop, bootstrap 95%
interval, severity monotonicity, and the $5\times 5$ sensitivity matrix. Human
pairwise validation is metric-specific and blinded. VNSM additionally reports
cross-family judge agreement and human correlation. Agreement below
Krippendorff’s $\alpha=0.6$ prevents a standalone VNSM claim.

B.2 Evidence-Seeking Harness

At most three $4\times 4$ contact sheets are generated per video with sampling
step

$\Delta=\max\!\left(1,\Delta_{\rm user},\left\lceil N/(16S_{\max})\right\rceil\right),\qquad S_{\max}=3.$ (22)

Tiles carry source-frame and timestamp labels, while a sidecar stores FPS,
duration, sampling step, and sheet count. Representative inputs are shown in
Appendix E . Read-only mounts isolate the evidence available
to each metric. SCF sees script and narration, while VNSM sees aligned narration
and rendered frames/clips. TPR sees plan and realized timeline. LHC sees ordered
health inputs and asset digests. ERY/PCE see delivery probes and accounting.
The network-free harness may call only bounded FFmpeg (editing and probing),
PySceneDetect (scene detection), Whisper (ASR), PaddleOCR (OCR), frozen
detectors, and manifest/diff readers. Tools expose evidence but never scores,
and every call is archived.

Appendix C Experimental Protocol and Reporting

C.1 External Baseline Contract

Task-matched native systems receive the same frozen script, source pool,
duration, aspect ratio, and budget while retaining their released orchestration
and tools. Adapters may translate serialization and export native artifacts,
but may not retrieve assets, rewrite narration, invent plans, add manual edits,
or perform unavailable repairs. We record the plan, call log, artifacts, final
video, failure code, time, and cost. A missing native artifact makes its
trace-dependent metric N/A. Systems requiring semantic adapter work enter the
task-adjacent track and are excluded from the headline matched ranking.
The five component-metric entries average five independent runs over the same
100 held-out tasks, with $\pm$ denoting the standard deviation across runs. Overall
Score instead averages the five reported component means and is shown without
an uncertainty estimate. It is N/A when any component mean is unavailable.
Missing components are neither imputed nor omitted from the denominator.

C.3 Depth and Real-Tool Diagnostics

For a subset of tasks, the evaluation protocol injects a registered late-stage
fault and records the repair branch $\tau^{+}$ . The shared case ID links the
script, shot plan, original execution, delivered video, and changes made during
repair, allowing recovery behavior to be inspected alongside normal production.

For horizon $H_{i}$ and first failure turn $t_{i}^{\rm fail}$ ,

$\operatorname{FailRate}@k=\frac{\sum_{i}\mathbf{1}[H_{i}\geq k]\mathbf{1}[t_{i}^{\rm fail}=k]}{\sum_{i}\mathbf{1}[H_{i}\geq k]}.$ (23)

Completion and repair at depth $k$ use the same eligible denominator. The
proxy–real gap is a paired mean between frozen-fixture replay and production
execution. Unmatched or failed runs are retained in reporting. These depth and
proxy–real diagnostics complete the registered protocol. The experiment tables
report the five registered metrics and the descriptive Overall Score.

Appendix F Limitations

WeVisCut is limited by the current HyperFrames template library, so uncommon
visual languages may be approximated using the closest available structure,
motion, and style. Expanding the library through automatic template generation could
broaden the design space. The dataset and project-disjoint split cannot cover
all domains, editing conventions, runtimes, or asset distributions, while
verified replay guarantees executability only within the registered runtime.
The quality of long-form videos also depends on the stability of the video
retriever, particularly for instruction-based retrieval. Integrating a trained
video generation model could reduce this dependency and improve robustness.
Finally, VNSM relies on a VLM. Although evidence isolation, frozen anchors,
agreement reporting, and human calibration improve auditability, they cannot
eliminate judge bias or service drift, motivating broader blinded studies and
cross-family audits.
