WeVisForensics: Self-Evolving Agent for Omni AI-Generated Video Detection Yipeng Du 1,2,* , Yuxi Li 2, † \dagger , Ying Zhang 2 , Chong Sun 2 , Chen Li 2, ‡ \ddagger , Jing Lyu 2 , Ying Tai 1, ‡ \ddagger Affiliation: [ Affiliation: [ Abstract 
Recent advances in video generation call for reliable detection grounded in explicit forensic evidence. However, current methods primarily depend on single-pass chain-of-thought reasoning that overlooks brief or localized artifacts, or on predefined multi-agent workflows that offer limited flexibility in selecting relevant inspections. Even with adaptive investigation, optimizing the investigation policy alone does not directly improve the forensic capabilities of fixed experts. We introduce WeVisForensics , an agentic framework in which a main investigator agent coordinates a self-evolving library of forensic inspector sub-agents spanning visual and audio cues. The investigator learns which artifact type to examine, over which window, and when to conclude, and these interactions provide shared feedback for both levels of agents. Our dual-timescale policy optimization strategy, DTPO , closes this loop through fast reinforcement learning of the investigator and slower refinement of the inspectors, guided by annotated artifact intervals, to report subtle artifacts they once dismissed. Furthermore, we curate ForenCue-30K , combining role-specific forensic annotations and interaction trajectories to supervise individual inspections and their coordination, with an accompanying reinforcement-learning pool. Experiments show that WeVisForensics averages 95.2% detection accuracy across in-domain, out-of-domain and agentic benchmarks, 9.0% above the strongest prior detector, while grounding its verdicts in localized evidence. 1 Introduction 
Rapid advances in video generation have improved the visual fidelity, temporal coherence, and realism of synthetic content, bringing new possibilities to media creation. The same progress makes fabricated recordings increasingly difficult to distinguish from authentic ones, raising concerns over misinformation, impersonation, and public trust. Reliable AI-generated video detection is therefore becoming an essential component of media forensics, where decisive cues span visual, textual, and audio dimensions and call for role-specific inspection (Figure 1 ). 
To meet this need, recent studies [ 44 , 45 , 28 , 20 , 36 , 15 , 34 , 23 , 18 , 33 , 56 , 30 , 46 ] use multimodal large language models (MLLMs) either to reason about authenticity in natural language or to coordinate expert analysis. However, as illustrated in Figure 12 , these approaches still face two challenges in how forensic evidence is examined and used. a) Holistic single-pass reasoning. CoT-centric methods [ 36 , 20 , 28 , 44 , 45 ] judge the whole video in one pass, with one model covering every artifact type. Artifacts confined to a brief interval or a small region are easily missed or explained away by hallucinated rationales (Figure 6 a), and claims entangled in one free-form trace cannot be checked or supervised individually. b) Static workflows or coordinator-only learning. Agent-based detectors either run a fixed expert pipeline [ 46 , 30 , 17 ] or train only the coordinator over fixed specialists. ARGUS [ 56 ] collects four expert reports and freezes the observers while training the judge, so irrelevant reports can outweigh the one that finds the artifact (Figure 6 b). Tool-augmented detectors [ 33 , 47 ] instead learn when to invoke tools, yet keep tool backends fixed. Both remain bounded by the local accuracy of frozen sub-agents, since an inspector that misses a subtle cue limits what better routing or aggregation can recover (Figure 6 , right). 
To overcome these challenges, we introduce WeVisForensics (Figure 2 ), an agentic paradigm that couples adaptive evidence collection with a self-evolving library of forensic inspector sub-agents coordinated by a main investigator agent along two complementary designs. 1) Adaptive forensic analysis. The Forensic Sub-agent Library (FSL) holds trainable inspectors that examine visual appearance, physical consistency, commonsense plausibility, and on-screen text, while audio tools inspect the soundtrack when one is present. Each call specifies a forensic role and a temporal scope, and the selected inspector returns findings that inform subsequent reasoning. The investigator uses these observations to request further inspections or issue an authenticity verdict, so evidence collection adapts to each video beyond a predefined workflow. 2) Investigator-inspector self-evolution. Adaptive dispatch still relies on what each inspector can detect, so the inspectors must also improve from the calls they serve. Yet because the investigator decides which inputs the inspectors encounter while their responses shape its decisions, updating both on every batch makes each a moving target for the other. Our DTPO ( D ual- T imescale P olicy O ptimization) therefore trains both from shared trajectories on two timescales (Figure 3 ). The investigator’s inspection policy is updated on every rollout batch using outcome rewards and call-level feedback. The inspectors accumulate the calls issued by the investigator, keep those grounded in annotated artifact intervals, select responses with interaction feedback for privileged distillation, revise their skill cards of reporting rules, and periodically refresh the FSL. The refined inspectors then return to subsequent rollouts, so the agent evolves from its own environment. 
Moreover, existing forensic video datasets [ 44 , 15 , 20 ] supervise a single detector with a verdict or a holistic rationale, providing neither role-specific evidence for sub-agents nor trajectories for coordinating them. We therefore curate ForenCue-30K , which decomposes forensic supervision by role. Each record isolates one forensic perspective and specifies a verdict, a confidence, and the temporal interval of the artifact, so that a sub-agent learns to report what its role can verify, including when the evidence is inconclusive. Agentic trajectories are assembled from executed sub-agent calls rather than imagined observations, so every decision is conditioned on evidence the main agent would actually receive. An accompanying RL pool supports DTPO. Since existing benchmarks score only the final verdict, we build AgenticCue , an agentic benchmark that groups generated videos by forensic category and evaluates both detection accuracy and whether an agent invokes the relevant sub-agents.
Our contributions are threefold: 
• 
We propose DTPO , an RL algorithm in which the investigator and its inspectors co-evolve from shared interactions on fast and slow timescales. Inspectors refine their weights and skill cards on the very calls the investigator issues, so its rollouts become their curriculum. 
• 
We present WeVisForensics , an agentic video forensics framework in which a main agent adaptively dispatches inspections to the FSL. The invocation strategy and sub-agent competence are jointly learned through DTPO. 
• 
We curate ForenCue-30K , a role-structured dataset with agentic trajectories covering diverse generators, and introduce AgenticCue , the first agentic forensic benchmark that evaluates both accuracy and call precision. 
2 WeVisForensics Agent Framework 
WeVisForensics treats AI-generated video detection as an investigation rather than a single act of perception. A human forensic analyst rarely judges a video from one glance: they form a hypothesis from the overall content, zoom in on the moment and the region where it can be tested, consult the appropriate specialist, and keep collecting evidence until the conclusion is supported. WeVisForensics turns this workflow into an agent system with two levels. A main agent, the investigator , plans the investigation, and a Forensic Sub-agent Library (FSL) of inspectors examines the video from complementary forensic perspectives and returns structured evidence. 
The framework is built on three design principles. (i) Separation of planning and perception. The investigator decides what to check and where , while each inspector decides whether the artifact of its specialty is present in the requested scope. Planning thus scales with the complexity of a video, and perception remains specialized and verifiable. (ii) Role-decomposed evidence. Every inspection returns a verdict, a calibrated confidence, and a visibly grounded explanation for a single forensic perspective. Each piece of evidence can therefore be audited, supervised, and credited on its own, instead of being entangled in one free-form rationale. (iii) Evolvable components. The parts of the system whose competence depends on the changing distribution of generators, namely the investigation policy and the visual inspectors, are trained to improve from the system’s own interactions, whereas measurement-like tools stay fixed and provide a stable reference. 2.1 Investigation Protocol Observation and actions. 
An investigation starts from a global overview of the video: frames sampled at approximately 1 FPS across the full duration and uniformly reduced to at most 16, each tagged with its absolute timestamp, together with the duration and an indicator of whether an audio stream is present. At every turn, the investigator emits a single structured message consisting of a reasoning field and an action. The reasoning states the current hypothesis, what the evidence gathered so far does or does not establish, and why the next inspection is informative. Before the final answer, it synthesizes all returned evidence, including contrary and inconclusive findings. The action either calls one tool with its arguments, a temporal window [ t s , t e ] [t_{s},t_{e}] for the window-based inspectors, a single timestamp for OCR, and no arguments for the whole-track audio tools, or terminates the investigation with a real/fake verdict.

Audio tools. 
Generated videos increasingly carry synthesized speech, and an inconsistency between what is heard and what is seen is often more conspicuous than any visual artifact. The voice profile tool performs speaker diarization, separates overlapping speech, and estimates for each speaker an age, female/male/child probabilities, and arousal, dominance, and valence scores. The report is descriptive by design: a strong age or gender conflict with a clearly visible speaking face is useful supporting evidence, whereas dubbing, voice-over, and multiple speakers must be discounted. The ASR analysis tool transcribes the soundtrack with word-level timestamps and asks a frozen language model to review only concrete transcript and timing anomalies, such as robotic, uniformly spaced segments, against natural patterns such as hesitations and breathing pauses. Both tools are invoked on videos with an audio stream, typically when a speaking face or a talking-head scene is visible. Trainable and fixed members.

Visual artifacts are the cues that shift most rapidly as generators improve, and the calls issued by the investigator expose exactly the inputs, scopes, and difficulty levels on which the inspectors are consulted. The audio tools and the PaddleOCR detector remain fixed: their outputs are measurements, such as speaker attributes, transcripts, and text boxes, whose meaning should not drift while the investigator learns to interpret them, and the scarcity of labeled audio forgeries would make their adaptation unreliable. The investigator itself is optimized with reinforcement learning, so the system improves both in what it asks and in what it is told. 
3 Data Construction 
Built from 30,000 curated videos, ForenCue-30K provides multi-level supervision for our framework, comprising 117,552 role-specific records for the sub-agents, 33,896 interaction trajectories for the main agent, and a 7,500-row RL pool for DTPO (Figure 4 and Table 2 ). Its construction follows three principles: broad generator coverage , a role-aligned forensic taxonomy , and temporally grounded evidence annotation . 
Generated videos are primarily curated from public datasets (GenBuster-200K [ 44 ] , RewardData [ 7 ] , VBench-2.0 [ 55 ] , and AIGVDBench [ 24 ] ) covering open-source and earlier-generation commercial models, supplemented with videos generated through AI video-generation platforms and specialized forgery domains including digital humans and speaker-mismatched audio forgeries. The generated training videos include CogVideoX [ 52 ] , HunyuanVideo [ 16 ] , LTX-Video [ 10 ] , EasyAnimate [ 49 ] , Wan 2.1/2.2 [ 38 ] , Gen-2/3, Pika 1.5, Kling 1.0/1.5, MiniMax-Video-01, Veo 3, and Vidu Q1. 3.1 Role-Grounded Supervision Construction 
Supervision is constructed in a pipeline that mirrors the structure of the agent (Figure 4 ). Each curated video is first annotated separately for every forensic role, the annotations are then converted into training records for the inspectors, and executed inspector calls are finally assembled into investigation trajectories for the investigator. Every stage keeps the same role taxonomy, so the evidence an inspector learns to report is exactly the evidence the investigator learns to request and interpret. Role-structured taxonomy. 
Instead of one holistic rationale per video, supervision is organized by forensic perspective and subdivided into fine-grained artifact dimensions. Matching the six FSL roles, we categorize artifacts into Visual appearance (26,378 records), Physical consistency (32,348), Common sense (40,246), OCR consistency (18,580), ASR analysis , and Audio profile . Visual appearance covers texture and detail integrity, boundary blending, ghosting, and the consistency of lighting, shadows, and reflections. Physical consistency covers body and object structure, kinematics, object permanence, contact, and fluid or deformable dynamics. Common sense covers causality, affordances, contextual roles, and event order, and OCR consistency covers glyph structure and the temporal stability of rendered text. The audio roles cover the plausibility of speech timing and the agreement between the voice and the visible speaker. Each record isolates one perspective and specifies a verdict (issue, no-issue, or inconclusive), a confidence, an explanation, and the temporal interval of the artifact. Real videos contribute records under the same format, so every role sees both outcomes. This balance teaches each inspector to report only what its role can verify, rather than to equate “generated” with “an artifact of my type is visible”. Evidence annotation. 
For each video and role, a rubric-conditioned VLM annotator examines the timestamped frames under a role-specific guideline listing the artifact dimensions above and source-provenance constraints, localizing observable artifacts within temporal intervals and writing frame-grounded explanations of what is visibly wrong. Explanations must refer strictly to observable visual evidence, and roles without visible anomalies are recorded as no-issue even on generated videos, preventing forced attribution. A cross-family VLM verifier then checks each candidate against the video and refines or rejects it, filtering hallucinated explanations, over-generalized commonsense claims, and imprecise intervals. Human reviewers inspect sampled annotations per source and role during prompt development and source onboarding, and their findings are fed back into the guidelines and filtering rules, while audio inconsistencies are annotated directly by reviewers who watch each clip with sound (Appendix B ). Trajectory synthesis. 
Interaction trajectories are constructed through execution-grounded synthesis. Given the video overview and verified role evidence as training-only context, a VLM trajectory planner decides which roles and temporal scopes to consult, modeling how an expert investigation prioritizes relevant checks. Every planned call is then executed by the corresponding inspector, and the planner writes the reasoning of each turn conditioned on the reports actually returned, so that the investigator never learns from imagined observations. All training-only metadata are stripped from student-facing messages, conditioning each decision strictly on inference-time observations. Trajectories cover both real and generated videos, use about two calls on average under the four-call budget, and reach 96.0 % 96.0\% appropriate tool selection in a human audit of 100 trajectories. RL pool. 
The name ForenCue-30K refers to the core video supervision set, while expanded annotation records, trajectories, and the accompanying RL pool are counted separately (Figure 4 ). The RL pool contains 7,500 rows spanning 15 collections and 56 source subsets, with 3,750 unique real and 3,180 unique generated clips repeated with source-dependent weights to balance labels and sources. Each generated clip carries its role-specific artifact intervals, which ground the selection of inspector calls in the slow process, and each clip carries a verified set of relevant roles, which defines the tool-coverage reward (Sec. 4.2 ). Quality control. 
Export checks validate record structure, role-specific fields, video duration, and the availability of sampled frames. For interaction trajectories, construction checks match call identifiers to stored responses and enforce the action budget and the protocol rules of Sec. 2.1 , and automated text checks screen the student-facing messages for training-only metadata leakage. Each trajectory record retains the video and source identifiers, sampled-frame timestamps, ordered messages, and target verdict. 
3.2 AgenticCue Benchmark 
Existing benchmarks score only the final verdict, so an agent that reaches the right answer through irrelevant inspections, or that cannot handle a particular class of artifacts, remains invisible. AgenticCue is designed to expose both. Unlike the training set, which is dominated by open-source and earlier commercial models, it targets newer-generation commercial releases (Veo 3.1, Kling 2.5/3.0, Seedance 1.5/2.0, Hailuo 2.3/H3, Wan 2.6, Sora 2, Jimeng, and HappyHorse 1.0) alongside held-out digital-human, face-swap, and voice-mismatch audio forgeries, and thus also measures generalization to generators unseen during training. Category construction. 
The benchmark spans five forensic categories, Visual , Physical , OCR , Common sense , and Audio , each containing exactly 500 generated videos verified to exhibit that category’s artifact. Candidate clips are first annotated by model annotators and then confirmed by human reviewers. A clip may belong to several categories, and 170 clips do, giving 2,330 unique generated videos. All categories share the same 500 real videos, so each category forms a balanced set of 1,000 videos. None of its videos appear in ForenCue-30K. Metrics. 
For each category, we report binary detection accuracy on its balanced set, counting invalid or missing outputs as errors. To evaluate whether the agent consults the right specialist, we additionally report invocation precision, the fraction of generated videos on which the agent invokes tool s s that actually call for role s s . The reference set of relevant roles for the four visual tools comes from dense annotations in which two cross-family VLM annotators independently mark every role worth inspecting on each generated video and a label-blind judge resolves disagreements, while for audio it is the Audio category. 4 Training Pipeline 
WeVisForensics is trained in two stages. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on ForenCue-30K first teaches the investigator the investigation protocol and each inspector its role-specific reporting format (Sec. 4.1 ). DTPO then lets the investigator and the inspectors improve together from their own interactions (Sec. 4.2 ), and a scalable implementation keeps the joint training practical (Sec. 4.3 ). Every model is adapted with low-rank adapters on a shared backbone, so that the investigator and all inspectors can be loaded and served as one backbone plus a few lightweight adapters, and both stages update every model in the same parameterization. 
4.1 Stage I: Supervised Initialization 
All models share the Qwen3.5-4B [ 31 ] backbone. The investigator and the four visual inspectors are trained as five separate rank-64 LoRA [ 11 ] adapters ( α = 128 \alpha{=}128 ) on all linear layers of the language model, and the backbone, the vision encoder, and the vision-language projector are frozen throughout, so that adaptation concentrates on forensic reasoning rather than low-level visual features.

Inspectors. 
Each visual inspector is trained on its own portion of the 117,552 role-specific records, extended with records on additional videos. The inspectors follow the same adapter recipe as the investigator, with a maximum length of 32,768 tokens (16,384 for OCR, whose input is a single frame). The resulting system consists of a single backbone plus five small adapters, which makes it easy to store and serve. 
4.2 Stage II: DTPO 
DTPO makes interaction experience the link between learning to request evidence and refining the inspectors that provide it. The investigator determines which inputs the inspectors encounter, and the resulting reports influence both its subsequent decisions and which interactions are selected for inspector training. Updating both at the same time would make each a moving target for the other. The fast process therefore updates only the investigator on every rollout batch, while the slow process accumulates role-specific interaction queues over multiple batches and aligns the inspectors with a slowly changing EMA teacher [ 37 ] . The updated FSL then supplies observations for later rollouts, closing the loop. Video-level labels and artifact annotations are used only during training. At inference, the learned investigator and inspectors execute the protocol of Sec. 2.1 with fixed parameters. Fast process: investigator policy learning.

Skill-card evolution. 
Adapter updates change how an inspector perceives. Some failure modes, however, are better expressed as explicit reporting rules, for example that motion blur on fast-moving hands is not a structural defect, or that a soft object deforming under direct contact is not a physics violation. Alongside its adapter weights, each trainable inspector therefore carries a skill card [ 39 ] , a short list of Report and Ignore rules appended to its role instruction and seen by student and teacher. After each distillation round, a frozen reflector LLM reviews recent calls that the current inspector still judges wrongly, with their labels, and proposes rule edits. A candidate card replaces the current one only if it yields more correct verdicts on held-out recent calls without losing any correct verdict on either real or generated videos. This acceptance test makes card edits monotone on recent experience and prevents the reflector from trading false alarms for misses. Examples of evolved cards are given in Appendix D . Synchronizing fast and slow updates. 
Training alternates rollout collection, investigator optimization, interaction selection, and periodic FSL refinement. Each batch is collected with the current main policy and a fixed inspector snapshot. After the investigator update, selected interactions enter role-specific queues. Every H H investigator steps, a synchronization barrier checks whether each queue contains enough examples to update its inspector. Eligible students are distilled, their skill cards are revised, and the new FSL snapshot is published before rollout collection resumes, while roles with insufficient examples retain their current parameters and keep their queues for the next barrier. This schedule lets the investigator learn between FSL updates while each refinement stage draws on accumulated interaction experience. 
4.3 Scalable Implementation 
A single DTPO step runs 32 × G 32\times G multi-turn investigations, each invoking up to four inspectors on video inputs, so inspector serving and FSL refreshes dominate the cost of training. The investigator and the four visual inspectors are served by separate vLLM engines that share the training cluster, and the rollout workers execute investigations asynchronously, so that a slow audio or OCR call does not stall other trajectories in the batch. The audio tools and PaddleOCR run as independent services. At each synchronization barrier, the four inspector refreshes are packed into two parallel lanes of GPUs, one handling visual appearance followed by common sense and the other physical consistency followed by OCR, which balances the per-lane workload because the roles accumulate very different numbers of calls. Refreshed adapters are merged into the backbone weights and published to the inference engines as full checkpoints rather than hot-swapped as LoRA modules, which we found to be numerically more robust on our hardware. Skill-card revision runs after publication. With a refresh every 20 steps, the full joint schedule of a few hundred investigator steps completes in about a day on our cluster.

6 Related Work 
6.1 AI-Generated Video Detection 
Early detectors treat the task as binary classification over generation traces in images [ 41 , 27 , 43 , 35 ] and videos [ 2 , 5 , 54 , 13 , 22 ] , outputting a score without verifiable evidence, and benchmarks such as GVD [ 2 ] , GenVideo [ 5 ] , GenVidBench [ 26 ] , and AIGVDBench [ 24 ] scale up binary evaluation across generators. Building on MLLM-based image forensics [ 14 , 53 , 21 , 51 , 12 ] , recent video detectors pair each verdict with a natural-language account of the observed artifacts. Methods such as BusterX [ 44 ] , BusterX++ [ 45 ] , and VidGuard-R1 [ 28 ] train this reasoning with reinforcement learning, while Skyra [ 20 ] , VideoVeritas [ 36 ] , and VidForensics-M1 [ 23 ] strengthen it with artifact grounding, perception pretext tasks, and temporal-grounding rewards, respectively. IVY-FAKE [ 15 ] and Omni-Fake [ 18 ] provide annotated data for explainable detection, while OmniVL-Guard [ 34 ] unifies detection and grounding across text, images, and videos. However, these detector-centered formulations do not learn when to request a specialized follow-up inspection or how to adapt it to intermediate observations. 
6.2 Agent-Based Forensic Reasoning 
General video agents query specialized models to gather evidence over long inputs [ 42 , 48 , 19 ] , while forensic agents organize analysis through explicit tools or specialized roles. Tool-augmented detectors such as ClueAegis [ 4 ] , OmniVL-Guard Pro [ 33 ] , and TUE-Detector [ 47 ] train a single MLLM to invoke specific toolchains or visual operations such as cropping, zooming, and segmentation. Multi-agent methods such as SafeGuard [ 46 ] , Hermes [ 17 ] , and DVAR [ 30 ] instead distribute evidence extraction, verification, and deliberation across specialized agents. ARGUS [ 56 ] and Deepfake-Agent [ 9 ] further train a judge or aggregator over fixed expert agents. However, the invoked experts in these designs never learn from the interactions they serve while the coordinating policy is trained, leaving the experts’ forensic response policies outside the ongoing policy-learning loop. 
7 Conclusion 
This paper presents WeVisForensics, an agentic framework for AI-generated video detection in which an investigator coordinates a self-evolving library of forensic inspectors. Within this framework, DTPO is introduced to optimize the investigator on a fast timescale while refining the inspectors from its calls on a slow one. To support training and evaluation, ForenCue-30K is constructed with role-specific supervision and executed trajectories, and AgenticCue is established to assess both detection verdicts and the inspections behind them. Extensive experiments across benchmarks demonstrate the effectiveness of WeVisForensics, highlighting the value of co-evolving the investigator with its inspectors.

Appendix A Implementation Details Training and inference setup. 
The main agent and the four visual sub-agents are rank-64 LoRA adapters on a shared Qwen3.5-4B backbone, initialized from SFT adapters trained on 33,896 interaction trajectories and 117,552 role-specific records, while the base Qwen3.5-4B serves as the frozen CLUE reference p ref p_{\mathrm{ref}} . The main agent observes up to 16 uniformly sampled frames of the full video, and each window-based visual sub-agent inspects its requested window at 2 FPS, whereas the OCR sub-agent inspects the frame at its requested timestamp and the two audio tools analyze the whole soundtrack in Sec. 2.2 . In the fast process, GRPO samples G = 4 G{=}4 rollouts per video with 32 videos per batch and at most 4 sub-agent calls per trajectory for one epoch over the RL pool, and the main-agent learning rate warms up linearly to 1 × 10 − 5 1{\times}10^{-5} . Role-specific evidence annotations and main-agent interaction trajectories are produced by model annotators and coupled with human feedback (Figure 4 ) before entering the training records of Section 3 . Three patterns explain these gains. First, the physical sub-agent stops hedging. Its inconclusive rate falls on every benchmark, from 26.9% to 0.3% on GB, 12.3% to 0.9% on GB++, 10.5% to 1.2% on MintVid, and 6.4% to 0.4% on AgenticCue, and its F1 rises by 9.1–31.3%. Third, OCR evidence becomes sharper, with F1 rising on every benchmark and most on GB++ by 11.8%. Appendix D Learned Skill Cards 
This section lists the complete skill cards learned by the forensic library across four training runs. Each rule is appended to the user prompt of the corresponding sub-agent alongside the inspection query. Report rules specify generation artifacts to flag, while Ignore rules describe benign camera, motion, or compression effects that should not be mistaken for synthesis errors. The learned rules are local and contrastive. A typical Report rule describes an artifact against an intact reference in the same frame, such as a single fused glyph among sharp neighboring characters or melting facial contours against a sharp background, and the matching Ignore rule names the global degradation that produces a similar look, such as uniform blur or compression across the whole region. Because each edit is mined from calls that the current inspector still judges wrongly, the cards are specific to the failures of that run and differ in concrete content across runs. For example, the physical card of Run 2 targets finger articulation and garment topology, whereas that of Run 3 targets rigid tools sliding over granular surfaces and morphing sculptures.
