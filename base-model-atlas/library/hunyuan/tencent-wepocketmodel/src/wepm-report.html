WePM: Hierarchical Conflict Reconciliation for Unified MLLMs Unified Pocket Models for In-App Multimodal Intelligence Journal: gigabyte Abstract 
Bringing multimodal intelligence from the cloud to users’ devices keeps sensitive data local and substantially reduces cloud inference costs, but deploying a specialized model for every capability duplicates vision–language capacity under tight memory and compute budgets. In this paper, we first introduce and formalize the concept of heterogeneous capability density (HCD), which characterizes how broadly and effectively a model serves heterogeneous task families per unit of resident inference memory. Achieving high HCD across representation, generation, and decision tasks presents two fundamental challenges. First, these capabilities require visual evidence at different spatial granularities, yet must operate within a fixed token budget. Second, learning from separately collected task corpora requires coordinating potentially conflicting updates to shared parameters. Constructing cross-task paired supervision to support such coordination is costly and often ambiguous for open-ended inputs; directly combining task gradients, however, can lead to interference and imbalanced task contributions due to differences in gradient scale, loss weighting, and exposure frequency.
To address these challenges, we present WePM, a family of pocket models that unify multimodal representation, generation, and decision within a single vision–language backbone. Multi-Granularity Visual Budgeting (MGVB) allocates a fixed visual-token budget across global, regional, and fine-grained banks to reconcile heterogeneous evidence requirements.Unified Listwise Knowledge Distillation (ULKD) enriches separately collected representation and generation data with soft teacher guidance, avoiding costly cross-task paired annotations and arbitrary hard targets for open-ended inputs. Stable and Efficient Multitask Optimization (SEMO) regulates each task’s contribution to shared-parameter updates through explicit loss weights and exposure schedules, while sequential gradient accumulation executes the joint update within training-memory constraints. Built on these mechanisms, WePM uses task-appropriate paths to produce normalized embeddings, autoregressively decoded responses, and schema-constrained decisions.
For a direct comparison with existing unified methods, we first evaluate WePM in the shared joint representation–generation setting, where it outperforms all compared baselines across five evaluation groups and raises the Joint score from the strongest baseline’s 38.15 to 52.74. In single-task evaluations, WePM-4B achieves the highest average performance across all three capability families, scoring 72.86 over 30 embedding benchmarks, 62.41 over 39 generation benchmarks, and 61.11 over seven typed-decision benchmarks. These results demonstrate that WePM maintains strong and balanced task-specific performance while unifying heterogeneous capabilities. Compared with the separate-model stack with the highest HCD, WePM achieves slightly higher balanced performance with 61.9% less resident weight memory, resulting in a 2.64-fold higher HCD. Together, these results show that diverse multimodal capabilities need not come at the cost of a large stack of specialized models, positioning WePM as a promising foundation for capable on-device AI under tight memory constraints.

1.  Introduction 
One important direction for the next stage of AI is to bring intelligence from the cloud to users’ devices, helping users get things done in their daily lives while keeping sensitive data local and substantially reducing cloud inference costs. Such assistance spans heterogeneous capabilities, from finding and understanding personal content to producing useful responses and making context-dependent choices as interactions unfold. Yet, unlike cloud servers, on-device systems operate under tight constraints on system RAM, GPU memory, and compute. Deploying a separate specialized model for each capability duplicates vision–language capacity and inflates the resident resource footprint. This leads to a central question: how can a single compact model support more heterogeneous tasks, perform well on each of them, and remain within a limited on-device memory budget? In this paper, we investigate this question by unifying representation, generation, and decision within one on-device multimodal model. 
To formalize this objective, we first introduce the concept of Heterogeneous Capability Density (HCD), which characterizes the range and quality of capabilities that a model can expose per unit of resident inference memory. Let K K be the number of predefined task families that reach their respective performance thresholds, M M the total resident inference memory required to support these families, p i p_{i} the normalized performance of family i i , and w i w_{i} its application-specific importance. We define

where H w H_{w} is the weighted harmonic mean of the family-level scores. Task heterogeneity is defined at the capability-family level rather than the benchmark level: tasks that share the same fundamental output form and functional objective belong to the same family. For example, OCR and VQA are both treated as generation tasks and therefore do not separately increase K K . The weights can reflect an application’s demand for different capabilities; in this work, we assign equal weights to all families to encourage uniform coverage. HCD thus measures how broadly and effectively a model can serve an application within a fixed memory budget: a higher HCD indicates that the application can expose more capable functions without proportionally increasing its resident memory footprint. 
Achieving high HCD across heterogeneous tasks—such as representation, generation, and decision—is far from straightforward. These capabilities differ not only in their output semantics but also in the visual evidence and training signals they require. Representation maps independently encoded inputs to normalized embeddings whose similarities must remain stable across indexed items and future queries. Generation instead requires hidden states to remain sensitive to the current instruction, fine-grained visual evidence, and an autoregressively growing answer prefix. Decision evaluates the current state against caller-specified options or criteria and produces a constrained choice, ordinal score, or binary judgment. Unifying these capabilities within one compact backbone therefore introduces two fundamental difficulties: they compete for visual evidence at different spatial granularities, and their task-specific objectives can interfere when updating the same parameters. 
We organize these difficulties into a granularity conflict and a shared-optimization conflict. The granularity conflict concerns which evidence should be retained under a fixed visual-token budget: coarse semantics may suffice for broad retrieval, whereas hard-negative discrimination, detailed generation, and visually grounded decisions may require regional or fine-grained evidence. The optimization conflict arises because representation, generation, and decision data are typically collected in separate corpora and consumed through task-specific batches. An update that improves one objective can therefore degrade another, while unequal gradient scales and update frequencies can allow one task to dominate the shared parameters. Two natural responses expose further challenges. Constructing cross-task paired annotations leads to a supervision conflict, because such annotations are expensive and open-ended inputs may not admit a unique target in another output space. Integrating task gradients instead leads to a magnitude conflict, because each task’s effective contribution depends jointly on its native gradient scale, loss coefficient, and exposure frequency. 
To address these conflicts, we develop WePM, which coordinates shared capacity at the levels of visual evidence, task supervision, and optimization. Multi-Granularity Visual Budgeting addresses the granularity conflict by allocating a fixed visual-token budget across global, regional, and fine-grained banks, conditioning selection on the available text while retaining query-independent indexing. Unified Listwise Knowledge Distillation addresses the supervision conflict by transferring soft generation and representation preferences without requiring exhaustively paired annotations or imposing unique hard targets on open-ended inputs. Stable and Efficient Multitask Optimization addresses the magnitude conflict by explicitly controlling fixed loss coefficients and task exposure, while using sequential gradient accumulation to realize the specified joint update under constrained training memory. By sharing the visual encoder and multimodal backbone, the three paths avoid the duplicated resident capacity of separate models while preserving the output semantics required by each task.

2.  Unified Multimodal Models: Formulation and Tensions 
2.1  Existing Formulations 
A natural way to combine different tasks such as retrieval and generation is to reuse a generative backbone, define an additional embedding readout, and train the two task losses with a single weighted-sum objective. We take this shared-backbone construction as the baseline formulation of this paper: it is the common recipe behind prior unified models, the substrate that WePM retains, and the reference configuration in our experiments. Existing methods instantiate the representation readout differently: GRIT uses mean-pooled input states [ 66 ] , whereas VLM2Vec and E5-V extract last-token states for multimodal embedding [ 37 , 36 ] . Our baseline follows the latter design, projecting the final hidden state after multimodal prefill without inserting an additional representation token, while retaining standard causal decoding for generation.

2.2  Conflicts Under Shared Capacity 
Unifying heterogeneous tasks—such as representation, generation, and decision—within a compact shared backbone introduces two fundamental difficulties. First, the three capabilities require visual evidence at different spatial granularities, despite sharing the same limited visual-token budget. Second, their training data are typically collected in separate task-specific corpora. Existing unified training recipes therefore commonly alternate among task-specific batches, causing consecutive updates to optimize different output semantics over the same backbone parameters. We trace these two difficulties to a granularity conflict in the retained evidence and an optimization conflict in the shared parameter updates. 
Granularity conflict—which visual evidence should be retained? Representation may rely on global semantics for broad retrieval, but distinguishing hard negatives often requires regional attributes and fine details. Generation similarly combines scene-level context with localized evidence for OCR and detail-centric question answering, while decision may depend on either global state or local text, values, and regions specified by its schema. Because all three capabilities operate under a visual-token budget of at most B B , uniformly compressing an image can preserve evidence useful to one task while discarding evidence required by another. The first challenge is therefore to preserve task-relevant evidence at multiple granularities within a single fixed token budget. 
The second difficulty arises during optimization. Even when the inner product is nonnegative, unequal gradient norms and update frequencies can allow one task to dominate the shared parameters. There are two natural approaches to mitigating this interference, but each exposes a distinct challenge. 
Supervision conflict—can task-specific data be converted into paired supervision? One possible approach is to annotate every input with targets for all capabilities, so that representation, generation, and decision can be optimized from semantically aligned examples. Constructing such data at scale, however, is expensive and often fundamentally ambiguous. For example, a representation query such as “a small red flower” specifies a meaningful retrieval target but does not determine a unique generation response. Both “a small red flower blooms on the hillside” and “a small red flower grows in the woods” are plausible continuations. Assigning either response as the sole target introduces an arbitrary interpretation rather than genuine cross-task supervision. More generally, relevance relations, open-ended sequences, and typed decision labels define different target spaces and cannot always be converted into one another through hard annotations. The supervision challenge is therefore to obtain complementary cross-task learning signals without requiring exhaustive paired data or imposing a unique target where none exists. 
Magnitude conflict—which task determines the shared update? An alternative to constructing fully paired data is to integrate gradients from different task-specific batches into one logical update. However, gradient integration alone does not ensure balanced task influence. For n i n_{i} batches of task i i , coefficient λ i \lambda_{i} , and mean gradient g ¯ i \bar{g}_{i} , the resulting update is

The effective contribution of task i i is therefore determined jointly by its native gradient magnitude, its coefficient λ i \lambda_{i} , and its exposure frequency n i n_{i} . These quantities are not naturally comparable across heterogeneous objectives: representation losses are typically reduced over queries or candidate sets, generation losses over supervised tokens, and decision losses over typed instances. Their data pools may also differ substantially in size, causing some tasks to be sampled more frequently than others. Consequently, equal coefficients do not imply equal influence, and equal exposure does not imply balanced updates. A task with a larger effective contribution can dominate the joint gradient and overwrite capabilities learned from other tasks, even when their gradient directions are not directly opposed. Conversely, suppressing that contribution too aggressively can prevent the task from learning sufficiently. The magnitude conflict therefore lies in the mismatch between each task’s numerically induced optimization strength and its intended importance in the final capability trade-off. The challenge is to control both task weighting and task exposure so that the joint update reflects a deliberate balance rather than incidental differences in loss normalization, annotation density, or corpus size. 
These conflicts motivate the three corresponding components of WePM. MGVB reconciles heterogeneous evidence requirements within a shared visual-token budget. ULKD supplies cross-capability soft supervision without requiring unique hard targets or exhaustively paired annotations. SEMO controls the effective contribution of each task through explicit loss coefficients and task exposure, while using memory-efficient sequential accumulation to realize the specified joint update.

3.1  Multi-Granularity Visual Budgeting 
Representation, generation, and visually grounded decision require evidence at different spatial scales. Coarse-grained retrieval is largely governed by global scene semantics, whereas discriminating hard negatives often requires object-level and relational cues. OCR and detail-centric visual question answering depend on localized evidence that may occupy only a few image patches, while a decision may depend on either global state or a local value specified by its criteria. Under a fixed visual-token budget, uniform compression inevitably preserves redundant information at some scales while discarding evidence critical at others. We therefore introduce the Multi-Granularity Visual Budgeting , which explicitly distributes the available visual capacity across global, regional, and fine-grained representations. 
Given spatially indexed features X = { x j } j = 1 N X=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N} produced by a single vision-encoder forward pass, MGVB constructs three complementary token banks: 
V = [ V g ; V r ; V f ] , B g + B r + B f = B , V=[V_{g};V_{r};V_{f}],\qquad B_{g}+B_{r}+B_{f}=B, 
where B g B_{g} , B r B_{r} , and B f B_{f} denote the token budgets of the global, regional, and fine-grained banks, respectively, and B B is the total number of visual tokens supplied to the language backbone. The global bank employs learned latent queries to aggregate image-level semantics. The regional bank summarizes candidate regions, preserving object identities, spatial layouts, and inter-object relations. The fine-grained bank retains selected patch features that capture small objects, scene text, and other localized details that would otherwise be suppressed by regional aggregation. Together, these banks provide a compact yet scale-complementary visual representation. 
While the three banks determine which scales of evidence are retained, the accompanying text determines which content is most relevant. Accordingly, MGVB conditions token selection in the regional and fine-grained banks on the available textual context c c . where x global x_{\mathrm{global}} denotes the global image representation, and f r f_{r} and f f f_{f} are learnable scoring functions. The top-ranked regions and patches are retained under budgets B r B_{r} and B f B_{f} , respectively. For instance, a question concerning a small chart label favors the corresponding local patches, whereas a question about the overall trend emphasizes the chart structure. Importantly, this context-aware selection only reallocates capacity within a shared representation; it introduces neither task-specific visual encoders nor separate backbone branches. 
MGVB follows the same selection principle across the three capabilities whenever visual input is present. The context c c is instantiated by the text accompanying the image, such as a generation instruction, a retrieval query or caption, or a decision schema and its criteria. If no text is available, MGVB uses a query-independent context c ∅ c_{\emptyset} , ensuring that indexed representations do not depend on future queries. Finally, each retained token is augmented with granularity and spatial-position embeddings to encode its scale and spatial origin before being passed to the shared backbone. 
3.2  Unified Listwise Knowledge Distillation 
Retrieval and generation corpora provide complementary but typically unpaired forms of supervision. Retrieval examples contain relevance annotations but no reference responses, whereas generation examples provide target sequences without candidate-level relevance labels. A straightforward way to bridge this gap would be to annotate each example for both capabilities. As discussed in Section 2.2 , however, such cross-capability annotation is not only expensive but can also be fundamentally ambiguous. For example, a retrieval query such as “a small red flower” specifies meaningful relevance relations but admits many valid continuations rather than a unique generation target. Assigning one pseudo-response as the ground truth would impose an arbitrary interpretation on the query. 
To address this supervision conflict, we introduce Unified Listwise Knowledge Distillation , which transfers soft teacher preferences instead of constructing unique hard targets for missing annotations. ULKD retains the native supervision available in each corpus while supplementing partially annotated inputs with capability-specific distributional guidance. In the present formulation, ULKD bridges the representation and generation supervision spaces; the decision path continues to use its native schema-conditioned typed objective. 
ULKD places retrieval and generation distillation within a common distribution-matching framework using two frozen capability-specific teachers. In both cases, the teacher provides relative preferences over the corresponding choice set, and the student minimizes a KL divergence to the teacher distribution. This formulation preserves multiple plausible alternatives instead of collapsing supervision into a single hard token, response, or positive candidate. 
For generation, directly assigning a pseudo-response to an open-ended retrieval query would impose one arbitrary interpretation as the target. For a retrieval query, ULKD additionally samples an on-policy continuation and obtains token-level guidance from the generation teacher, while the embedding teacher transfers candidate-level ranking preferences and instance-level representations. ULKD therefore enriches task-specific data with complementary soft supervision without replacing their native objectives, requiring exhaustively paired annotations, or imposing a unique response on an open-ended query. 
3.3  Stable and Efficient Multitask Optimization 
The magnitude conflict identified in Section 2.2 arises because the effective influence of each task depends jointly on its native gradient scale, loss coefficient, and exposure frequency. A multitask optimizer must therefore determine not only how strongly each task is weighted, but also how often it contributes to the shared update. Adaptive weighting methods such as Fast Adaptive Multitask Optimization (FAMO) [ 49 ] adjust task coefficients according to recent loss reduction. For heterogeneous objectives, however, short-term loss progress need not reflect the desired trade-off in downstream capability, and estimating such progress requires post-update loss re-evaluation and additional forward passes. Moreover, adaptive coefficients regulate only one component of task influence; the exposure assigned to each task remains a separate design choice. We therefore introduce Stable and Efficient Multitask Optimization , which explicitly specifies both task weighting and task exposure while executing the resulting joint update through memory-efficient sequential gradient accumulation. 
Formally, let 𝒯 = { emb , gen , dec } \mathcal{T}=\{\mathrm{emb},\mathrm{gen},\mathrm{dec}\} denote the representation, generation, and decision tasks. where λ i \lambda_{i} remains fixed throughout training. Defining

the contribution of task i i to the joint update is n i , t ​ λ i ​ g ¯ i , t n_{i,t}\lambda_{i}\bar{g}_{i,t} . SEMO therefore makes the two controllable factors governing task influence explicit: λ i \lambda_{i} determines the per-batch weight, while n i , t n_{i,t} determines the task exposure. Both are specified deliberately rather than being implicitly determined by loss normalization or incidental sampling frequency. 
A simultaneous implementation of this objective would materialize the losses for all representation, generation, and decision batches before backpropagation, retaining their computation graphs at the same time. This execution strategy exceeds the available training memory in our setting. SEMO instead processes the constituent batches sequentially. Each batch performs a forward pass followed immediately by weighted backpropagation, after which its computation graph is released. Sequential accumulation therefore computes the gradient of the specified joint objective while requiring memory for only one batch graph at a time. 
SEMO separates the statistical design of the joint update—task coefficients and exposure frequencies—from its memory-efficient execution. It does not project or otherwise modify task-gradient directions; instead, it addresses the magnitude conflict by ensuring that each task’s effective contribution follows an explicit capability trade-off rather than uncontrolled differences in loss scale, annotation density, or corpus size. 4.  Experiments 
4.1  Experimental Details 
We initialize WePM from the pretrained Qwen3.5-4B/2B dense model [ 75 ] and fine-tune all parameters. Unless otherwise specified, WePM refers to this 4B model throughout the paper. We choose this scale because deployment profiling identifies it as approximately the practical upper bound for dense models on representative on-device platforms such as Apple M3: larger models degrade interactive performance and user experience. The maximum sequence length during training is 8,192 tokens, and the combined generation, embedding, and decision corpus contains approximately 100 million cleaned samples. 
Retrieval instances combine negatives from their original datasets with additional hard negatives mined using the WeMM-9B embedding model [ 122 ] . After negative expansion and cross-device aggregation, embedding training uses an effective global batch size of approximately 20k, exposing the model to more confusable candidates during contrastive learning. For ULKD, Qwen3.5-27B Dense [ 75 ] provides generation-side supervision, while WeMM-9B embedding [ 122 ] provides embedding-side supervision. These capability-specific teachers guide the shared student through their corresponding generation and representation objectives. 
During full-capability training, generation, embedding, and decision batches are sampled in a 7:5:1 ratio according to the data distribution. Their training losses are reduced per supervised token, per query, and per typed decision instance, respectively. The controlled representation–generation weighting ablations use a separate schedule of three embedding and five generation batches per logical update. The weights in Table 7 denote per-batch loss coefficients ( λ emb / λ gen \lambda_{\mathrm{emb}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{gen}} ), not task-exposure ratios; B B in Table 5 denotes the MGVB visual-token budget. 
For comparisons with existing unified methods and controlled ablations, we use a shared-capability protocol covering 16 representation and generation benchmarks in five groups. Image retrieval (ImageRet.) comprises MSCOCO image-to-text retrieval [ 47 ] , CIRR [ 56 ] , and VisDial [ 15 ] . Text retrieval (TextRet.) comprises ArguAna [ 98 ] , FiQA-2018 [ 60 ] , CQADupStack-Gaming [ 29 ] , and SCIDOCS [ 14 ] from BEIR [ 90 ] . Mathematical and scientific reasoning (Math) comprises MMMU [ 118 ] , MathVista [ 58 ] , and MathVision [ 100 ] . OCR and document understanding (OCR) comprises OCRBench [ 55 ] , ChartQA [ 63 ] , and TextVQA [ 84 ] . We reproduce all baseline results using the same input resolution and evaluation scripts as WePM. 
For both evaluation protocols, each benchmark score is reported using the benchmark’s standard evaluation metric. Each group score is the arithmetic mean of its benchmark scores. The Embedding score averages ImageRet. and TextRet., the Generation score averages Math, VQA, and OCR, and Joint averages the Embedding and Generation scores, giving the two capabilities equal weight. Higher scores are better throughout. The full-capability protocol separately evaluates representation, generation, decision, resident memory, and HCD. 5.  Positioning Against Existing Approaches 
5.1  Unified Representation–Generation Models and Distillation 
Contrastive vision–language pretraining, exemplified by CLIP [ 76 ] , established transferable representations for image–text matching. Recent work has extended generative vision–language backbones into general-purpose embedding models. VLM2Vec learns instruction-conditioned multimodal embeddings [ 37 ] , while UniME combines knowledge distillation with hard-negative training for universal multimodal retrieval [ 24 ] . A complementary line of research jointly supports retrieval and generation within one model. FROMAGe augments a frozen generative language model with image retrieval [ 39 ] ; GRIT combines embedding and generative instruction tuning [ 66 ] ; CAFe couples contrastive and autoregressive objectives [ 114 ] ; and MMGen trains a unified multimodal model for both embedding and generation [ 99 ] . GEM further connects generated query analysis with dense retrieval, although its scope is limited to text retrieval [ 82 ] . Retrieval and generation have also developed distinct forms of teacher supervision. RocketQAv2 performs dynamic listwise distillation between a dense retriever and a re-ranker [ 79 ] , while BiXSE learns from probabilistic graded-relevance targets for individual query–document pairs [ 93 ] . For language generation, GKD trains on student-generated sequences to reduce exposure mismatch between fixed training targets and student-visited states [ 2 ] ; subsequent on-policy distillation similarly evaluates continuations sampled from the student [ 57 ] . Cross-world on-policy distillation extends this principle to VLMs by supervising prediction changes under interventions to answer-relevant visual evidence [ 88 ] . 
WePM follows the shared-backbone paradigm but targets heterogeneous capability under compact-model constraints. Beyond coupling indexable embeddings with open-ended generation, it introduces schema-constrained typed decision within the same backbone. Its ULKD component brings retrieval and generation distillation into a common distribution-matching framework without conflating their prediction spaces: token probabilities supervise generation along student-visited prefixes, while candidate scores and embedding alignment transfer retrieval structure. By initiating generation rollouts from retrieval queries that lack reference answers, ULKD extracts complementary supervision from partially annotated inputs while retaining the original hard objectives. WePM therefore extends prior unified models through coordinated control of visual evidence, heterogeneous supervision, and update magnitude rather than by merely adding another output head. 
5.2  Typed Decision and Agentic Control 
Tool-using agents repeatedly select actions from the tools or operations available in their current state. ReAct interleaves language-model reasoning with environment actions [ 113 ] ; Toolformer learns when and how to invoke external APIs from self-supervised signals [ 81 ] ; and Gorilla focuses on selecting and calling from large API collections [ 74 ] . These approaches broaden language models from response generation to interactive control, but the selected action is typically expressed autoregressively as a tool name, argument sequence, or action string. The surrounding application must then parse and validate that generated output before execution. Typed decision models provide a complementary interface for cases in which the application already defines the valid output space. Jev receives application state together with typed questions and returns probability-bearing Choice, Score, or Noul results rather than free-form text [ 94 ] . This formulation separates bounded judgment from open-ended response generation: the caller specifies the alternatives or criteria, and the resulting label or score can be consumed directly by program logic. Such decisions can support tool routing, action selection, candidate assessment, and stopping conditions across agentic workflows. 
WePM adopts this typed-decision abstraction for on-device use. A schema-conditioned decision slot summarizes the shared multimodal state, and lightweight typed readouts score the caller-defined output space in a single forward pass. Unlike a standalone decision service, this path reuses the same compact vision–language backbone as representation and generation, reducing duplicated resident capacity while preserving a bounded interface for agent control. 
5.3  Visual Token Compression and Multi-Granularity Modeling 
High-resolution vision–language models employ several strategies to control the cost of long visual sequences. LLaVA-UHD partitions an image into variable-sized slices and compresses visual tokens while preserving spatial organization [ 110 ] , whereas Qwen2.5-VL adopts a native dynamic-resolution vision transformer with window attention [ 4 ] . TokenPacker injects fine-grained local features into compact coarse queries [ 45 ] , and VisionZip selects informative tokens before they enter the language model [ 112 ] . In contrast, inference-oriented approaches such as AdaVSkip reduce computation by bypassing selected transformer modules, without directly reallocating the input-token budget [ 89 ] . 
MGVB is most closely related to input-side visual token compression, but differs in how the retained capacity is organized. It explicitly partitions a fixed budget into global, regional, and fine-grained banks and conditions regional and patch selection on the accompanying text. When text is unavailable, as in offline gallery indexing, MGVB uses a query-independent context to prevent indexed representations from depending on future queries. This design targets heterogeneous evidence requirements across the three capabilities: global semantics support image matching, whereas OCR, detail-centric generation, and visually grounded decision may require localized visual evidence. 
5.4  Multitask Optimization under Memory Constraints 
Multitask learning is often affected by gradient interference and unequal optimization rates. PCGrad modifies gradient directions by projecting away conflicting components [ 116 ] ; FAMO adapts task weights according to relative loss progress [ 49 ] ; and LDC-MTL balances tasks through loss-discrepancy control [ 108 ] . These methods address complementary aspects of optimization interference. In compact heterogeneous multitask training, however, the update rule must additionally account for unequal task exposure and the memory cost of retaining heterogeneous computation graphs. 
SEMO addresses these practical constraints with fixed per-task coefficients, a predefined exposure schedule derived from the task pools, and sequential gradient accumulation. In our controlled comparison, this design provides a stronger joint trade-off than the tested progress-driven FAMO variants while avoiding their post-update loss re-evaluation. Sequential execution computes the specified joint gradient without retaining all task graphs simultaneously. SEMO is therefore complementary to general gradient-manipulation methods: it targets stable and memory-feasible execution across heterogeneous task pools rather than proposing a universal replacement for adaptive multitask optimization. 
6.  Limitations and Future Work 
WePM currently exhibits some degradation in agentic capabilities relative to its original Qwen backbone, as the current post-training pipeline primarily focuses on unifying multimodal capabilities without dedicated agent-oriented training. Dedicated post-training to preserve and strengthen tool-use and multi-step interaction capabilities alongside representation, generation, and typed decision has not yet been explored in this work. Multitask optimization also leaves room for improvement: SEMO relies on static analysis of the training-data mixture to determine task weights and exposure schedules, rather than adapting these configurations to evolving learning dynamics. Such dependence may limit its flexibility as task composition and data distributions change. 
Future work could explore three directions. First, task-adaptive parameter activation could allow the same model to use different amounts of active capacity according to task complexity and accuracy requirements. Tasks with less stringent requirements could obtain usable results from a subset of parameters, potentially reducing computation and improving response latency. Second, simplifying and extending post-training on top of WePM is important for practical adoption. Lightweight adaptation methods could enable users to incorporate task-specific data and refine targeted capabilities without repeating the full, complex multitask training pipeline or substantially degrading other capabilities. Third, reinforcement-learning methods tailored to unified models could jointly improve heterogeneous capabilities, with particular attention to restoring and strengthening agentic behavior while balancing task quality, cross-task interference, and on-device resource constraints. 
7.  Conclusion 
We introduce and formalize heterogeneous capability density (HCD) to characterize capability breadth and balanced task quality per unit of resident inference memory, and present WePM, a family of pocket models that unify multimodal representation, generation, and decision within a single vision–language backbone. MGVB reconciles heterogeneous evidence requirements by allocating a fixed visual-token budget across global, regional, and fine-grained banks. ULKD enriches separately collected representation and generation data with soft teacher guidance, avoiding costly cross-task paired annotations and arbitrary hard targets for open-ended inputs. SEMO regulates each task’s contribution to shared-parameter updates through explicit loss weights and exposure schedules, while sequential gradient accumulation executes the joint update within training-memory constraints. Together, these mechanisms support normalized embeddings, autoregressive responses, and schema-constrained decisions without duplicating the backbone across capabilities. In the joint representation–generation setting shared with existing unified methods, WePM outperforms all compared baselines across five evaluation groups and raises the Joint score from the strongest baseline’s 38.15 to 52.74. In single-task evaluations, WePM-4B achieves the highest average performance across all three capability families, scoring 72.86 over 30 embedding benchmarks, 62.41 over 39 generation benchmarks, and 61.11 over seven typed-decision benchmarks. Compared with the separate-model stack with the highest HCD, WePM achieves slightly higher balanced performance with 61.9% less resident weight memory, resulting in a 2.64-fold higher HCD (0.244 versus 0.092). These results demonstrate that a compact shared backbone can consolidate diverse multimodal capabilities while maintaining strong and balanced task-specific performance, positioning WePM as a promising foundation for capable on-device AI under tight memory constraints.
