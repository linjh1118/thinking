<!-- ==== ABSTRACT ==== -->
<section id="abstract" class="section">
  <div class="container narrow">
    <div class="section-heading reveal">
      <span class="eyebrow">Abstract</span>
      <h2>Can a VAE and its generative prior be trained together, directly?</h2>
    </div>
    <div class="abstract-block reveal">
      <p>
        Latent generative models typically follow a two-stage pipeline, training a variational autoencoder
        for reconstruction and then a generative model on the frozen latent space. Since
        reconstruction-optimized latents are not necessarily generation-friendly, jointly training both
        models is an appealing alternative. However, direct end-to-end training remains challenging, as it is
        prone to <strong>latent collapse</strong> and faces a <strong>generation&ndash;reconstruction
        conflict</strong>.
      </p>
      <p>
        In this work, we revisit this problem by analyzing how different objectives shape the latent space and
        identify two key insights. First, the <strong>entropy term in the KL objective is essential for
        preventing collapse</strong>: reconstruction and prior fitting both tend to shrink the posterior,
        while entropy preserves non-degenerate latent uncertainty. Second, reconstruction and generation
        exhibit <strong>asymmetric learning dynamics</strong>: reconstruction is fast and strongly supervised,
        whereas generation is slower and harder to optimize.
      </p>
      <p>
        Motivated by these insights, we achieve the first direct end-to-end training without latent collapse
        and further propose <strong>GenFirst</strong>, a simple generation-before-reconstruction strategy. The
        generative objective first shapes the latent space under weak reconstruction pressure, after which
        reconstruction is progressively strengthened to recover visual details. We validate this strategy with
        both continuous autoregressive priors with exact likelihoods and SiT priors with implicit likelihoods.
        With our end-to-end objective and GenFirst, SiT achieves a gFID of <strong>0.97</strong> with CFG
        and <strong>1.45</strong> without CFG on ImageNet 256&times;256, while MMDiT reaches a GenEval score of
        <strong>0.90</strong> on text-to-image generation.
      </p>
      <p>
        Beyond image generation, we further extend the framework to <strong>shared visual latents</strong> for
        generation and representation learning, and to <strong>continuous unified text&ndash;image
        generation</strong>. These results demonstrate the generality of stable end-to-end latent learning
        across generative priors and modalities.
      </p>
    </div>

    <figure class="paper-figure reveal mt-3">
      <img class="zoomable" src="static/images/fig1.png"
           alt="Comparison of latent generative modeling paradigms"
           data-caption="Figure 1. Comparison of latent generative modeling paradigms.">
      <figcaption>
        <b>Figure 1. Comparison of latent generative modeling paradigms.</b>
        (a) Conventional two-stage methods train the generative model on a fixed, reconstruction-optimized
        latent space that may be suboptimal for generation. (b) Naive end-to-end training lets the generative
        loss update the encoder, but causes latent collapse. (c) REPA-E avoids collapse by stopping the
        generative gradient and reshaping the latent space with an external representation-alignment loss, so
        the generative objective never directly shapes the latents. (d) Our method enables direct end-to-end
        training: the prior loss shapes a generation-friendly latent space while the entropy term prevents
        collapse.
      </figcaption>
    </figure>
  </div>
</section>

<!-- ==== ANALYSIS ==== -->
<section id="analysis" class="section is-soft">
  <div class="container">
    <div class="section-heading reveal">
      <span class="eyebrow">Analysis</span>
      <h2>Two insights behind stable end-to-end training</h2>
      <p class="lead">
        We revisit end-to-end training from the VAE objective rather than from the architecture, and find that
        both failure modes reported in prior work have a concrete, correctable cause.
      </p>
    </div>

    <div class="card-grid">
      <div class="card reveal reveal-delay-1">
        <span class="tag is-accent">Insight 1 &middot; why it collapses</span>
        <h3>A prior&ndash;entropy imbalance, not a broken gradient</h3>
        <p>
          For a standard Gaussian reference prior, the KL regularizer splits into a
          <b>prior-fitting</b> term and a <b>negative posterior-entropy</b> term:
        </p>
        <div class="eq-block" style="box-shadow:none; margin:.9rem 0;">
          \[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi(z|x)\|p_0(z)) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[-\log p_0(z)]}_{\text{prior fitting}} \;-\; \underbrace{\mathcal{H}(q_\phi(z|x))}_{\text{entropy}} \]
        </div>
        <p>
          Naive end-to-end training inherits the LDM recipe of a tiny KL weight
          (\(\lambda_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\approx\!10^{-6}\)) and then adds a learned prior loss on top. The added
          objective is a strong extra prior-fitting force while entropy stays suppressed:
          \(\lambda_{\mathrm{prior}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{KL}} \gg \lambda_{\mathrm{KL}}\). Posterior variance is
          driven to zero, the means concentrate on nearly one code, and the latent space collapses.
        </p>
        <div class="takeaway">
          The entropy term is <b>not a minor regularizer</b>: it is the counteracting force to learned prior
          fitting. Making it explicit turns latent collapse from a failure mode into a diagnosable imbalance.
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="card reveal reveal-delay-2">
        <span class="tag is-accent">Insight 2 &middot; why weighting is not enough</span>
        <h3>Reconstruction is fast, generation is slow</h3>
        <p>
          Reconstruction is a strongly supervised mapping problem: given latents, the decoder gets direct pixel
          supervision and recovers detail within few steps. Generation is a distribution-modeling problem: the
          prior must learn the global latent distribution before it can sample anything recognizable.
        </p>
        <p>
          Strong reconstruction pressure early therefore anchors the encoder to a reconstruction-oriented
          latent space that the prior can never catch up with, while permanent generative pressure prevents
          detail recovery. What matters is not only <b>how</b> the two objectives are weighted, but
          <b>when</b> each of them shapes the latent space.
        </p>
        <figure class="paper-figure" style="margin-top:1rem;">
          <img class="zoomable" src="static/images/fig2.png"
               alt="Asymmetric learning dynamics between reconstruction and generation"
               data-caption="Figure 2. Asymmetric learning dynamics between reconstruction and generation.">
          <figcaption>
            <b>Figure 2.</b> Reconstruction reaches good fidelity within far fewer steps, while generation
            needs a much longer optimization process to become sampleable.
          </figcaption>
        </figure>
      </div>
    </div>

    <figure class="paper-figure reveal mt-3">
      <img class="zoomable" src="static/images/fig3.png"
           alt="Qualitative effects of each objective on the latent posterior"
           data-caption="Figure 3. Qualitative effects of each objective on the latent posterior.">
      <figcaption>
        <b>Figure 3. What each objective does to the posterior.</b>
        Reconstruction spreads the posterior means (\(\mathrm{Std}(\mu)\!\uparrow\)) but shrinks the
        uncertainty (\(\mathbb{E}[\sigma^2]\!\downarrow\)), leaving gaps between codes that are hard to sample.
        Prior fitting without entropy shrinks both and collapses the posterior toward a constant code. Adding
        entropy restores \(\mathbb{E}[\sigma^2]\), increases posterior overlap and makes the latent space
        sampleable.
      </figcaption>
    </figure>

    <div class="mt-3">
      <details class="detail-block reveal">
        <summary>Three natural fixes for the generation&ndash;reconstruction conflict &mdash; none of them works</summary>
        <div class="detail-body">
          <figure class="paper-figure">
            <img class="zoomable" src="static/images/fig4.png"
                 alt="Latent dimension, VAE size and prior size ablations"
                 data-caption="Figure 4. Larger latent dimension, VAE capacity and prior capacity.">
            <figcaption>
              <b>Figure 4.</b> (a) A larger latent dimension tolerates a stronger prior weight, but even
              \(d\!=\!128\) only supports \(\lambda_{\mathrm{prior}}\!\approx\!0.1\), and makes the AR prior
              harder to model. (b) Scaling the VAE (deeper encoder/decoder, or decoder only) does not
              alleviate the conflict and can even hurt reconstruction. (c) Scaling the prior improves
              generation but pushes reconstruction further down &mdash; more shaping pressure, same conflict.
            </figcaption>
          </figure>
        </div>
      </details>

<!-- ==== METHOD ==== -->
<section id="method" class="section">
  <div class="container">
    <div class="section-heading reveal">
      <span class="eyebrow">Method</span>
      <h2>An entropy-preserving objective, and generation before reconstruction</h2>
      <p class="lead">
        One objective makes direct joint training stable; one schedule decides which objective gets to shape
        the latent space first.
      </p>
    </div>

    <div class="eq-block reveal">
      \[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{E2E}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{rec}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{LPIPS}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LPIPS}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{GAN}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GAN}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{prior}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}} - \lambda_{\mathrm{ent}}\mathcal{H}(q_\phi(z|x)) \]
      <p class="eq-note">
        Prior fitting and posterior entropy are <b>separated and separately weighted</b>. The prior term is
        whatever generative objective is being trained &mdash; an exact latent NLL for the continuous
        autoregressive prior, or the flow-matching velocity loss for SiT. For SiT we additionally keep the
        classic \(10^{-6}\) KL term purely to bound the latent scale, since an unbounded \(z\) would make the
        injected Gaussian noise relatively too small and open another shortcut.
      </p>
    </div>

    <div class="stage-grid">
      <div class="stage reveal reveal-delay-1">
        <span class="stage-tag">Stage 1</span>
        <h4>Generation first</h4>
        <p>
          A large prior weight (with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{ent}}=\lambda_{\mathrm{prior}}\)) lets the generative
          objective form a generation-friendly latent geometry, while reconstruction preserves image
          information and entropy prevents collapse. This stage is the long one: 500 epochs for EAR,
          80 epochs for EiT.
        </p>
      </div>
      <div class="stage reveal reveal-delay-2">
        <span class="stage-tag">Stage 2</span>
        <h4>Reconstruction refinement</h4>
        <p>
          The prior and entropy weights drop together &mdash; \(1\!\to\!0.25\) for EAR and
          \(0.1\!\to\!0.01\) for EiT &mdash; giving reconstruction more relative influence so that visual
          detail returns without dismantling the latent space formed in stage 1. This stage is short: 140
          and 40 epochs respectively.
        </p>
      </div>
      <div class="stage reveal reveal-delay-3">
        <span class="stage-tag">Then</span>
        <h4>Freeze and train the prior</h4>
        <p>
          For diffusion priors the end-to-end phase is followed by a prior-only phase: freeze the learned VAE
          and train a fresh SiT / MMDiT on its latents. The same frozen VAE also transfers to
          512&times;512 and to text-to-image without retraining the tokenizer.
        </p>
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="card-grid mt-3">
      <div class="card reveal">
        <span class="tag">Instantiation A</span>
        <h3>EAR &mdash; exact-likelihood autoregressive prior</h3>
        <p>
          Latents are flattened into \(N=hw\) continuous tokens and modelled by a causal Transformer with a
          \(K\)-component Gaussian-mixture head, following FARMER:
          \(p_\theta(z|c)=\prod_i p_\theta(z_i|z_{&lt;i},c)\). The exact likelihood makes the prior&ndash;entropy
          balance directly measurable, and the same loss both fits and reshapes the latent distribution.
        </p>
      </div>
      <div class="card reveal reveal-delay-1">
        <span class="tag">Instantiation B</span>
        <h3>EiT &mdash; flow-matching / diffusion prior</h3>
        <p>
          SiT for class-conditional ImageNet and MMDiT for text-to-image, trained with the linear-interpolant
          velocity loss \(\|v_\theta(z_t,t)-(z-\epsilon_t)\|^2_2\). No explicit likelihood over latents is
          available here, which is exactly why the entropy term (rather than a likelihood diagnosis) is what
          keeps training stable.
        </p>
      </div>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

<!-- ==== EXTENSION 1: SHARED LATENTS ==== -->
<section id="shared-latents" class="section is-soft">
  <div class="container">
    <div class="section-heading reveal">
      <span class="eyebrow">Extension experiment 1 &mdash; Shared latents</span>
      <h2>Toward a shared latent space for generation and representation learning</h2>
      <p class="lead">
        The first of two <b>extension experiments</b> that push the same recipe past text-to-image.
        If the generative objective can shape a latent space, representation learning can share it. We swap
        the FLUX.1-dev encoder for a Qwen3-VL ViT plus a posterior projector and add representation
        supervision <em>inside</em> the same end-to-end loop, so the features being aligned are exactly the
        latents the diffusion model consumes.
      </p>
    </div>

    <figure class="paper-figure reveal">
      <img class="zoomable" src="static/images/fig9ab.png"
           alt="Two ways of adding representation supervision to end-to-end training"
           data-caption="Figure 9 (a-b). Adding representation supervision inside end-to-end training.">
      <figcaption>
        <b>Figure 9 (a&ndash;b). Two ways to add representation supervision.</b>
        (a) Latent-level SigLIP supervision, so representation learning shapes exactly the latents the
        generative model uses. (b) Caption-based VLM-NLL supervision applied to the intermediate
        ViT features: they are fed to a frozen Qwen3-VL and the caption NLL is minimized &mdash; no in-batch
        negatives needed.
      </figcaption>
    </figure>

    <div class="table-wrap reveal mt-3">
      <table class="data-table">
        <caption><b>Table 9. Reconstruction, generation and representation quality of different visual
          encoders.</b> The FLUX.1-dev encoder is replaced by the Qwen3-VL ViT plus a posterior projector;
          Top-1 is an ImageNet linear probe.</caption>
        <thead>
          <tr><th>Encoder</th><th>Setting</th><th>rFID &darr;</th><th>PSNR &uarr;</th><th>GenEval &uarr;</th><th>DPG-Bench &uarr;</th><th>Top-1 Acc. &uarr;</th></tr>
        </thead>
        <tbody>
          <tr><td>FLUX.1-dev VAE</td><td>Pretrained</td><td class="best">0.18</td><td class="best">31.59</td><td>&ndash;</td><td>&ndash;</td><td>3.1</td></tr>
          <tr><td>Qwen3-VL ViT</td><td>Pretrained</td><td>&ndash;</td><td>&ndash;</td><td>&ndash;</td><td>&ndash;</td><td>79.64</td></tr>
          <tr class="ours"><td>Qwen3-VL ViT + projector</td><td>E2E</td><td>0.42</td><td>27.79</td><td>0.89</td><td>83.70</td><td>77.71</td></tr>
          <tr class="ours"><td>Qwen3-VL ViT + projector</td><td>E2E + SigLIP supervision</td><td>0.39</td><td>27.64</td><td class="best">0.90</td><td class="best">83.76</td><td>79.25</td></tr>
          <tr class="ours"><td>Qwen3-VL ViT + projector</td><td>E2E + VLM-NLL supervision</td><td>0.46</td><td>27.19</td><td>0.89</td><td>83.49</td><td class="best">81.69</td></tr>
        </tbody>
      </table>
    </div>
    <p class="table-note reveal">
      Representation supervision can be folded into end-to-end training almost for free: caption-based
      VLM-NLL supervision lifts linear-probe accuracy from 77.71 to <b>81.69</b>, which even
      <i>exceeds</i> the pretrained Qwen3-VL ViT&rsquo;s 79.64, while GenEval and DPG-Bench stay at
      0.89 / 83.49.
    </p>

    <div class="takeaway reveal mt-3">
      Generation and representation learning can live in <b>one</b> latent space &mdash; no separate tokenizer
      for the diffusion model and no frozen encoder for the probe.
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

<!-- ==== EXTENSION 2: UNIFIED MMDIT ==== -->
<section id="unified-mmdit" class="section">
  <div class="container">
    <div class="section-heading reveal">
      <span class="eyebrow">Extension experiment 2 &mdash; Unified MMDiT</span>
      <h2>Unified text-image modeling via an end-to-end diffusion</h2>
      <p class="lead">
        The second <b>extension experiment</b> shows the framework is not tied to pixels. Here we replace one
        modality outright: text is encoded into continuous latents as well, and a single block-causal MMDiT
        learns both directions between the two latent spaces.
      </p>
    </div>

    <figure class="paper-figure is-narrow reveal">
      <img class="zoomable" src="static/images/fig9c.png"
           alt="Unified text-image modeling via end-to-end diffusion"
           data-caption="Figure 9 (c). Unified text-image modeling via stable end-to-end diffusion.">
      <figcaption>
        <b>Figure 9 (c). Unified text&ndash;image modeling with a block-causal diffusion transformer.</b>
        Continuous text and image latents are jointly optimized for bidirectional text-to-image and
        image-to-text generation; entropy terms keep either latent space from collapsing while reconstruction
        losses keep both decodable.
      </figcaption>
    </figure>

    <div class="card reveal mt-3">
      <span class="tag is-accent">Setup</span>
      <h3>Two latent spaces, one diffusion backbone</h3>
      <p>
        Text is encoded into continuous latents by a text VAE (initialized from Cola) and reconstructed with a
        CE loss; text and image latents then enter a block-causal MMDiT trained on \(p(i|t)\) and \(p(t|i)\)
        with equal probability. Both diffusion directions shape the latent representations, the entropy term
        prevents collapse, and GenFirst balances latent modeling against reconstruction. Following the
        text-to-image recipe, the end-to-end phase uses a block-causal <b>MMDiT-L/2</b> (24 blocks, width
        1024) and the prior-only phase trains a fresh block-causal <b>MMDiT-XL/2</b> (28 blocks, width 1152)
        from scratch. Because of compute limits, the end-to-end phase uses half the steps of our text-to-image
        run and the prior-only phase only 30.8% of its samples.
      </p>
    </div>

    <div class="table-wrap reveal mt-3">
      <table class="data-table">
        <caption><b>Table 10.</b> Frozen versus end-to-end trained text/image VAEs, with the same
          block-causal MMDiT and the same three-stage recipe. Image-to-text is evaluated on the COCO
          Karpathy test split with <code>pycocoevalcap</code>.</caption>
        <thead>
          <tr>
            <th>Setting</th><th>GenEval &uarr;</th><th>DPG-Bench &uarr;</th>
            <th>CIDEr &uarr;</th><th>BLEU-4 &uarr;</th><th>METEOR &uarr;</th><th>ROUGE-L &uarr;</th><th>SPICE &uarr;</th><th>CLIPScore &uarr;</th>
          </tr>
        </thead>
        <tbody>
          <tr><td>Frozen text VAE + frozen image VAE</td><td>79.88</td><td>63.96</td><td>20.03</td><td>5.36</td><td>13.72</td><td>29.01</td><td>8.03</td><td>53.21</td></tr>
          <tr class="ours"><td>E2E-trained text VAE + frozen image VAE</td><td>81.87</td><td>68.24</td><td>30.19</td><td>7.46</td><td>16.81</td><td>32.95</td><td>11.12</td><td>55.96</td></tr>
          <tr class="ours"><td>E2E-joint-trained text VAE and image VAE</td><td class="best">86.57</td><td class="best">72.68</td><td class="best">33.28</td><td class="best">7.76</td><td class="best">17.74</td><td class="best">34.16</td><td class="best">11.81</td><td class="best">57.97</td></tr>
        </tbody>
      </table>
    </div>
    <p class="table-note reveal">
      Both arms use the same MMDiT and the same sampling configuration, so the gaps come entirely from how the
      latent spaces were trained.
    </p>

    <figure class="paper-figure reveal mt-3">
      <img class="zoomable" src="static/images/fig10.jpg"
           alt="Qualitative comparison of the three latent-space settings"
           data-caption="Figure 10. Qualitative comparison of the three latent-space settings.">
      <figcaption>
        <b>Figure 10. The three latent-space settings, same MMDiT and sampling configuration.</b>
        Top: text-to-image on DPG-Bench &mdash; only the jointly trained latents render the cat rather than a
        third teddy bear, and place the Opera House, Eiffel Tower and a snow-capped Everest together.
        Bottom: image-to-text on the COCO Karpathy test split &mdash; captions go from incoherent to
        &ldquo;children playing soccer in a field&rdquo;.
      </figcaption>
    </figure>

    <div class="takeaway reveal mt-3">
      Shaping the <b>text</b> latent space end-to-end is worth +1.99 GenEval and +10.2 CIDEr on its own;
      shaping <b>both</b> latent spaces jointly adds another +4.70 GenEval, +4.44 DPG-Bench and +3.1 CIDEr.
    </div>
  </div>
</section>
