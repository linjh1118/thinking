q002 - Marriott vs. Wyndham Loyalty Programs

What the question asks

This is not asking "which hotel company is better" in general. It asks how much customer demand flows through each loyalty program and how each loyalty program is funded. Then it asks which program is more likely to become a durable economic advantage.

Rollout, in plain English

The model looked up the FY2025 10-K filings for Marriott (MAR) and Wyndham (WH), parsed the filings, searched inside them for loyalty-program language, and pulled out the exact operating metrics:

  • Marriott: about 75% of U.S. hotel room nights and about 68% of global room nights were booked by loyalty members.
  • Wyndham: over 53% of U.S. check-ins and over 37% of global check-ins came from Wyndham Rewards members.
  • Marriott's loyalty program is funded partly by hotel/franchisee reimbursements and significantly by co-branded credit-card agreements.
  • Wyndham Rewards is funded by contributions from eligible revenues generated by loyalty members and collected from hotels in the system.

Beginner finance interpretation

A loyalty program is valuable because it can reduce dependence on online travel agencies, increase direct bookings, improve customer data, and make customers more likely to stay inside the brand family. Marriott wins this comparison because it has higher penetration and a more monetizable funding model.

The credit-card piece matters a lot. A hotel loyalty program funded by co-branded card economics can become a standalone profit engine, not only a marketing expense. Wyndham's program is still valuable, but the disclosed funding mechanism is more like a hotel-system contribution pool.

q003 - EA vs. Take-Two Competitors

What the question asks

This is a disclosure-comparison question. It asks who EA and Take-Two say they compete with, including both direct gaming competitors and non-gaming entertainment competitors.

Rollout, in plain English

The model found both companies' FY2025 10-K filings, parsed the business sections, and searched for "competition" disclosures. It noticed an important difference:

  • EA describes competitors generically: console, PC, mobile game companies, large diversified companies, new entrants, and other entertainment options such as movies, TV, social media, casual entertainment, and music.
  • Take-Two names specific competitors: EA, Embracer, Playrix, Playtika, Roblox, Savvy Games, Tencent, Ubisoft, and platform holders such as Sony, Microsoft, and Nintendo. It also names non-interactive entertainment such as motion pictures, TV, social networking, online apps, and short-form video.

Beginner finance interpretation

For public-company analysis, the level of disclosure can matter. A more detailed competitor list can help investors understand where the company thinks the real strategic pressure is. Here, Take-Two's disclosure is more granular because it names direct competitors and categorizes competitive threats.

q004 - CRWD vs. PANW Two-Year Revenue CAGR

What the question asks

This asks which cybersecurity company grew revenue faster from FY2023 to FY2025 and by how many basis points.

Rollout, in plain English

The model used SEC XBRL company facts to fetch revenue for CrowdStrike (CRWD) and Palo Alto Networks (PANW) for FY2023 and FY2025. Then it used the finance calculator for CAGR:

CAGR = (Ending revenue / Beginning revenue)^(1 / number of years) - 1

The values used were:

  • CRWD revenue: $2,241.236M in FY2023 to $3,953.624M in FY2025.
  • PANW revenue: $6,892.700M in FY2023 to $9,221.500M in FY2025.

The answer:

  • CRWD two-year CAGR: 32.82%.
  • PANW two-year CAGR: 15.67%.
  • Difference: about 1,715 basis points.

Beginner finance interpretation

CAGR is the "smoothed annual growth rate." It answers: if the company grew at the same rate each year, what annual rate would turn the starting revenue into the ending revenue?

Basis points are just hundredths of a percent:

1.00% = 100 basis points
17.15% = 1,715 basis points

CRWD grew much faster, even though PANW had larger absolute revenue.

q006 - Home Depot vs. Lowe's Inventory Efficiency

What the question asks

This asks which retailer turns inventory faster in FY2024. The key metric is Days Inventory Outstanding, or DIO.

Rollout, in plain English

The model gathered each company's cost of sales, beginning inventory, ending inventory, and actual fiscal-year day count from filings and XBRL facts. Then it calculated:

Average inventory = (Beginning inventory + Ending inventory) / 2
DIO = Average inventory / Cost of sales * Actual fiscal days

The answer:

  • Home Depot: 371 fiscal days, average inventory $22,213.5M, DIO 77.60 days.
  • Lowe's: 364 fiscal days, average inventory $17,151.5M, DIO 111.89 days.
  • Home Depot turns inventory faster by about 34.29 days.

Beginner finance interpretation

DIO means roughly "how many days of inventory is sitting in the business before being sold." Lower is usually better because it means less cash is trapped in inventory. Home Depot's lower DIO implies it converts inventory into sales much faster than Lowe's.

q011 - Target vs. Walmart Fixed Charge Coverage

What the question asks

This compares Target and Walmart using Fixed Charge Coverage Ratio (FCCR). It asks which company has more earnings cushion to cover interest and fixed rent.

Rollout, in plain English

The model found operating income, depreciation and amortization, interest expense, and fixed operating lease cost. It also handled the lease nuance: Target's operating lease cost included short-term and variable lease components, so those had to be removed; Walmart's $2,347M figure was already the fixed operating lease cost.

Formula:

EBITDAR = Operating income + D&A + Fixed operating lease cost
Total fixed charges = Interest expense + Fixed operating lease cost
FCCR = EBITDAR / Total fixed charges

The answer:

  • Target EBITDAR: $9,056M; fixed charges: $920M; FCCR: 9.84x.
  • Walmart EBITDAR: $44,668M; fixed charges: $4,592M; FCCR: 9.73x.
  • Target is slightly stronger by about 0.12x.

Beginner finance interpretation

FCCR asks: for every dollar of unavoidable financing-like cost, how many dollars of earnings-before-rent-and-D&A does the company generate? Both companies are strong here. The interesting part is not that Target is vastly safer; it is that Target is slightly better on this specific coverage metric.

q013 - Baker Hughes Buying Chart Industries

What the question asks

This is a precedent-transaction analysis. It asks for acquisition premium, strategic segment mix impact, cost synergies as percentages of Chart's financials, and a short deal-rationale summary.

Rollout, in plain English

The model used SEC filings, 8-K exhibits, merger proxy material, market price data, and calculators. It traced the deal terms to the Baker Hughes/Chart announcement materials and used Chart's standalone FY2024 financials.

Core answer:

  • Offer price: $210 per share.
  • Chart unaffected close on 7/28/2025: $171.65.
  • Premium: $210 / $171.65 - 1 = 22.34%.
  • Baker Hughes IET non-oil-and-gas revenue mix moves from about 15% standalone to about 30% combined.
  • Run-rate cost synergies: $325M.
  • Synergies as percentage of Chart FY2024 revenue: 7.81%.
  • Synergies as percentage of Chart FY2024 SG&A: 59.37%.

Beginner finance interpretation

In M&A, the premium tells you how much extra the buyer is paying over the undisturbed stock price. Synergies are expected cost savings or revenue benefits from combining companies. Expressing synergies as a percent of revenue or SG&A tests whether the synergy target is small, large, or wildly aggressive.

Here, the model correctly tied the math to the strategy: Baker Hughes was buying Chart to increase exposure to industrial and energy technology markets outside traditional oil and gas.

Insight
  • M&A questions often mix three layers: deal terms, market data, and strategic logic.
  • A good rollout does not stop at "premium = X"; it verifies the unaffected price date and then explains why the buyer is paying.
  • Synergy percentages are a sanity check: $325M sounds abstract, but nearly 60% of SG&A is a large operational claim.

q014 - IonQ Buying SkyWater

What the question asks

This asks whether IonQ's acquisition of SkyWater is valued like an ordinary semiconductor foundry, like strategic domestic manufacturing, or like a quantum vertical-integration premium.

Rollout, in plain English

The model gathered the merger announcement, SkyWater's FY2025 results, balance sheet data, and used calculators for valuation multiples.

Core answer:

  • Equity value: about $1.8B.
  • SkyWater FY2025 revenue: $442.1M.
  • SkyWater FY2025 adjusted EBITDA: $53.2M.
  • Equity-value / revenue: about 4.07x.
  • Equity-value / adjusted EBITDA: about 33.83x.
  • Adding net debt gives enterprise value around $2.0B, or about 4.52x revenue and 37.60x adjusted EBITDA.

Beginner finance interpretation

A valuation multiple compares price to a business metric. A mature foundry with modest margins usually would not command a roughly mid-30s EBITDA multiple. So the model concluded the price mainly reflects IonQ's desire to own manufacturing capability for its quantum roadmap. Domestic manufacturing value is relevant, but not enough by itself to explain the multiple.

q015 - Pfizer Buying Seagen

What the question asks

This asks for implied equity value, enterprise value, and EV/revenue multiple for Pfizer's cash acquisition of Seagen.

Rollout, in plain English

The model found the March 13, 2023 Seagen 8-K for the deal price and the FY2022 10-K for shares outstanding, cash, short-term investments, debt, and revenue.

Core math:

Equity value = offer price per share * shares outstanding
Enterprise value = equity value + debt - cash and short-term investments
EV / revenue = enterprise value / FY2022 revenue

The answer:

  • Offer price: $229.00 per share.
  • Shares outstanding: 186,789,367.
  • Equity value: about $42.77B.
  • Cash and short-term investments: about $1.735B.
  • Long-term debt: $0.
  • Enterprise value: about $41.04B.
  • FY2022 revenue: $1.962B.
  • EV/revenue: about 20.91x.

Beginner finance interpretation

Equity value is what shareholders receive. Enterprise value is closer to what the buyer is paying for the operating business, because it adjusts for debt and cash. Since Seagen had a lot of cash and no long-term debt, enterprise value is lower than equity value.

q017 - Kraft Heinz Adjusted EBITDA and Cash-Adjusted Margin

What the question asks

This asks the model to reconstruct Kraft Heinz's non-GAAP adjusted EBITDA bridge from GAAP net income, then remove the equity-award compensation add-back to calculate a more cash-like margin.

Rollout, in plain English

The model found the Q4/FY2024 earnings release filed as an 8-K exhibit and searched the reconciliation schedule. It pulled each line item in the bridge: net income, interest, other income/expense, taxes, D&A, license income, restructuring, hedges, impairments, and equity-award compensation.

Core answer:

  • Company-reported adjusted EBITDA: $6,363M.
  • Net sales: $25,846M.
  • Reported adjusted EBITDA margin: 24.62%.
  • Equity award compensation add-back: $109M.
  • Cash-adjusted EBITDA: $6,254M.
  • Cash-adjusted margin: 24.20%.
  • Difference: about 42 basis points.

Beginner finance interpretation

Adjusted EBITDA is management's version of earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization, with extra items added back. But add-backs can make profitability look better. Stock/equity compensation is non-cash today, but it is still economically costly because it dilutes shareholders. Removing that add-back gives a stricter cash-adjusted margin.

q019 - VSE 2026 Guidance Implied Revenue and EBITDA

What the question asks

This asks the model to use guidance ranges, take midpoints, calculate implied 2026 revenue and adjusted EBITDA, then compare the implied margin to historical margins in basis points.

Rollout, in plain English

The model found VSE's Q4 2025 earnings release and extracted guidance:

  • Revenue growth: 19% to 23%, midpoint 21%.
  • Adjusted EBITDA margin: 16.8% to 17.3%, midpoint 17.05%.
  • FY2025 revenue: $1,112.275M.

Then it calculated:

  • 2026 implied revenue: $1,112.275M * 1.21 = $1,345.853M.
  • 2026 implied adjusted EBITDA: $1,345.853M * 17.05% = $229.468M.

It compared 17.05% to historical adjusted EBITDA margins:

  • +382 bps vs. FY2023.
  • +217 bps vs. FY2024.
  • +60 bps vs. FY2025.

Beginner finance interpretation

Guidance often comes as a range. Taking the midpoint is a standard way to turn management guidance into a single model input. The "basis point comparison" shows whether management is implicitly guiding to margin expansion or margin compression. Here, VSE is guiding to continued margin expansion.

q023 - Intel 2024 Restructuring Plan

What the question asks

This is a disclosure-analysis question. It asks how Intel's restructuring plan flowed through the financial statements and how to reconcile three different numbers that can easily be confused:

  • total FY2024 restructuring and other charges,
  • severance/benefit charges attributable only to the 2024 plan,
  • cumulative 2024 plan cost disclosed in Q3 2024.

Rollout, in plain English

The model used Intel's FY2024 10-K, Q3 2024 10-Q, and FY2023 10-K. It searched the restructuring footnotes and human-capital disclosure, then calculated the roll-forward and headcount decline.

Core answer:

  • FY2024 total restructuring and other charges: $6,970M.
  • FY2024 employee severance and benefit charges attributable solely to the 2024 plan: about $2.2B.
  • Cumulative 2024 plan cost disclosed in Q3 2024: $2.8B.
  • 2024 plan severance liability roll-forward: $2,306M accruals, $2,004M cash payments, $302M ending accrued liability.
  • Headcount declined from 124,800 to 108,900, a reduction of 15,900 employees, or about 12.74%.

Beginner finance interpretation

The key lesson is that similarly named restructuring numbers can refer to different scopes:

  • The income-statement line is broad and includes severance, litigation, and impairments.
  • The 2024-plan-only severance number is narrower.
  • The cumulative plan cost includes severance plus certain non-cash impairments.

The model got this right because it did not collapse all "restructuring" words into one number. It separated the accounting line item, the plan-only severance component, and the cumulative plan cost.

How to Read These Rollouts Yourself

For each question, use this order:

  1. Read the question and identify the required output fields.
  2. Open runs/exp1_full27_round1/judge_report.md and check the rubric criteria.
  3. Open runs/exp1_full27_round1/results.json for the final answer and tool usage.
  4. Trace the tool pattern: filings first, source search second, calculations last.
  5. Ask whether every number in the answer can be tied back to a source or a formula.

The most reusable finance patterns in these correct examples are:

  • Growth: (ending / beginning)^(1 / years) - 1
  • Margin: profit metric / revenue
  • Basis points: percentage-point difference times 10,000
  • Enterprise value: equity value + debt - cash
  • Coverage: earnings-like numerator divided by fixed obligation denominator
  • Guidance beat/miss: (actual - guidance) / guidance
  • Adjusted EBITDA bridge: start from GAAP net income and add/subtract disclosed reconciling items
